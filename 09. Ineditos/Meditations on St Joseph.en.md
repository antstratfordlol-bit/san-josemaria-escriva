## Meditations on St Joseph In Joseph’s school {#inedito.meditations-on-st-joseph}

With great love, from the start of the Work, we have called St Joseph our Father and Lord. I have to say that he is even more than that: he is the Teacher of the interior life. And you and I need this interior life in order to be faithful in our external activities. What does interior life mean, but to dwell in constant dialogue with God and with the Mother of God? And who had a closer relationship with God and with our Blessed Lady than St Joseph? Every day I am deeply moved by a prayer I say before Mass, one of the preparatory prayers the Church recommends priests to say: O felicem virum, beatum Ioseph… O Joseph, happy and fortunate man, to whom was granted not only to see and to hear that God whom many kings longed to see and did not see, longed to hear and did not hear; but to carry him in his arms, to kiss, and clothe, and care for him.1 And you know that in the vast sea of God’s Trinity and Unity, wherever one Person is to be found the others are necessarily present too. Therefore St Joseph, who was on such familiar terms with God the Son, had an equally familiar relationship with God the Father and God the Holy Spirit.

St Joseph’s mission The Holy Patriarch was not an old man, but young, energetic and hardy, a great lover of loyalty, a man who was strong. Holy Scripture describes him with a single word: “just”.2 Joseph was a just man, filled with all virtues, as was proper for the man who was to be God’s protector on earth. At first he is taken aback to discover that his Immaculate Spouse is with child. He sees the hand of God in what has happened, but he doesn’t know what to do. And being upright, so as not to put her to shame, he decides to send her away quietly. But an angel of the Lord speaks to him in a dream, saying: “Joseph, son of David, do not fear to take Mary your wife, for that which is conceived in her is of the Holy Spirit; she will bear a son, and you shall call his name Jesus.”3 Just imagine how relieved Joseph must have been to hear the angel’s words! How happily he would have obeyed! Immediately, “when Joseph woke from sleep, he did as the angel of the Lord commanded him; he took his wife.”4 How beautiful! Joseph, spouse of Mary, a human being, is chosen to take the place of the father of the Son of God. Commenting on this passage, a Father of the Church places these words on the angel’s lips: “Although he is of the Holy Spirit, do not imagine that you are left outside the working of this divine plan. Although you have had no part in his begetting, since the Virgin has retained her virginity intact, nevertheless, all that pertains to the name of father, while safeguarding the honour of virginity, that do I give you, such as giving the child a name. It is you who will name him. For though this is not your

Thus, beginning with the giving of the name, I unite you closely with him who is to be born.”5 Let us fix our eyes on the Holy Patriarch St Joseph in order to understand his mission with regard to Christ more fully. In a way, Joseph is hidden – Sacred Scripture hardly mentions him. His mission is divine: he is completely dedicated, completely committed to looking after Christ. And is this not the path, my child, that God has marked out for you and me? Like Joseph, we too are dedicated souls, completely committed to God’s service, and seeking to identify with Christ in everything. Our only aim is to resemble Christ, to become completely identified with him. Mihi enim vivere Christus est,6 for me, to live is Christ. St Joseph could say that too. We should all be able to say it. Take a look at how you behave, at how you are leading your ordinary, hidden life. Perhaps you will ask me: “Father, how can I tell if I’m following Christ, if I’m really becoming identified with him?” And I will answer: “If you seek him every day, if you strive hard to find him, if you love him, then indeed you are living by him.” And I seem to hear your immediate reply: “Father – it was love that brought me to the Work!” It’s true, my son. The love of Christ has brought us to Opus Dei. But don’t forget that man’s will is weak and easily inclined toward complacency. True love, on the other hand, has a very clear characteristic: it is a gift of self and does not seek selfish satisfaction; it is founded on sacrifice and expressed in deeds of service. We have to love Jesus non verbo neque lingua, sed opere et veritate,7 not just with words, but above all with deeds.

Loving with deeds I could tell you lots of stories of things which have affected souls deeply. I remember, for example, a priest I knew many, many years ago. I never really got to know him properly. He was just a poor man, but he loved Jesus Christ. One day he was giving Holy Communion through the grille to some enclosed nuns. That priest had a contemplative soul, and he was reciting the words prescribed for distributing Holy Communion: Corpus Domini nostri Iesu Christi custodiat animam tuam in vitam aeternam.8 At the same time he was speaking with his heart to our Jesus, hidden under the sacramental species, saying to him: “Lord, I love you more than these do.” And our Lord replied to him, without any external words: “Love means deeds, not sweet words.” At that divine reproach the poor man was at first frightened, but it was followed by a sense of peace: a great joy and peace after that loving “complaint” from Christ. My very dearest child, you are a soul dedicated to God, to Jesus. What are you up to? Do you love with deeds, deeds that are habitually small? Because only very infrequently do we have the opportunity to serve him in great things. Normally great deeds only happen in our imagination. You will perform great

5 St John Chrysostom, Homilies on St Matthew’s Gospel, 4, 12 6 Phil 1:21 7 1 Jn 3:18 8 May the body of our Lord Jesus Christ keep your soul unto life everlasting: Roman Missal of St Pius V, Ordinary of the Mass 2 deeds too, when the opportunity comes, if you are faithful every day in the little things, if you become like a child before God. I have often seen a little toddler going with his mother to the fields and picking one little wildflower after another. He gathers flowers that you and I don’t even notice. He steps into puddles and searches under bushes to seek out little humble violets, flowers full of fragrance, which he gathers until he makes a posy, and offers it to his mother, who gives him such a loving look! My son, these things I am telling you are not silly sentimentalism, but upright and manly. If I offer you this comparison, it’s because in the presence of God our Father we can’t behave in any other way. Before the Divine Majesty you and I are like tiny tots. Our everyday lives are seemingly always the same; but if we see ourselves as little children before God, we will see that our day is filled with wonders, always different and always enchanting, because they are beautified by love. This is what God is asking of you: that hidden violet, your smile, your charity toward your brother, your hidden and fruitful sacrifice.

Self-giving to others How often have I spoken to you about proselytism…! Because the desire for souls, the zeal to bring others to Christ, is absolutely essential to our vocation, and is a necessary consequence of our love for God and for the Work. And I’ve very, very often said that I don’t dispense any of my children from this sweet obligation. Well, listen: the best proselytism is to make sure that none of your brothers gets lost, that we all make it to the end of the road marked out for us by God. It’s so painful to lose, not just one’s clothing, but a portion of one’s heart, in a puddle along the way! When one of my children loses his way it’s possible that a good portion of the blame lies with those who lived with him and didn’t come to his aid. When a group go for a trip in the country, or in the snow, if one of the party gets lost the others look for him frantically and make every sacrifice to find him. They take a thousand risks, but they come to his rescue and lead him back to the right path. Otherwise they would be a total disgrace. Well, you have to do just the same for your brothers, and afterwards for other people too. This is a matter of justice: firstly, I insist, toward our own brothers. Help them, encourage them. Forward! To do this, we have fraternal correction, just like the first of the Church’s faithful, and our prayer for one another, and our individual efforts to overcome our own defects, our desire to be faithful and send the others wholesome, healthy blood, full of oxygen. We have the same resources as the Apostles and the early Christians: faith, given by God, with hope and charity: supernatural virtues. Then, doctrine and the sacraments. And as a clear expression, the sacrament of Penance, spiritual direction and sincerity when speaking. When I was younger I had a great devotion to the forty martyrs of Sebaste. Those soldiers, because they were Christians, were condemned to be submerged in a pool of icy water. When they were about to be put to death they prayed: “Forty of us entered into battle: forty crowns we ask of you.” But one of them got out of the pool, whereupon the Holy Spirit moved one of their persecutors to get in and take his place, and become a witness to Christ. Well, we have to do the same for our brothers. We have to give ourselves to others. We have to give our lives for the people God has placed at our side, in the first place for those who have received the same divine call. The aspiration which comes straight from the Apostle’s very soul, mihi enim vivere Christus est,9 for to me to live is Christ, is joined with that other, et mori lucrum,10 and to die is gain. It is not a matter of physically losing one’s life, although for you and me death is not a matter of great importance. To die would be too comfortable, when there is so much to be done here on earth, when there are so few workers toiling for the Lord here below! Instead, think about dying a little each day; think about how we can each deny ourselves, put ourselves out in one matter after another, cheerfully, to make life more pleasant for your brother apostles. My son, don’t ever forget this motivation, if you want to be victorious in the struggle against your defects. Like that you will help others to be victorious too, and you’ll be contributing to their effectiveness.

Responsibility Today thousands of people, daughters and sons of God in Opus Dei, are renewing the gift of themselves which, one day, they made to the Work. They come from all walks of life: university professors, politicians, factory workers, farm hands, domestic employees… Some are household names, while others, the majority, are like Joseph, completely unknown. They will be celebrities in heaven! They are all equally important, because they are all crazily in love with God, and they know that another expression of dedication to God is a sense of responsibility. We should each feel the weight of our mission, being faithful wherever we find ourselves, as docile instruments of God, so that this family of ours, this army that is becoming universal, may respond with a single heartbeat in pursuing the Church’s goals, ut castrorum acies ordinata,11 like an army of peace in battle array. How happy it makes me whenever the Church asks us to take part in some apostolic project, because I know that my children will carry it out with a lot of love for God and a sincere sense of responsibility, cheerfully and humbly, conscious of being instruments of unity and effectiveness. Do you realise that you can help your brothers by sending them arterial blood; or on the contrary be a hindrance, like a landslide falling across their path? It would be bad enough if you yourself were to get lost; but it would be far worse to think that you could be a brake on the work of the others. What is there in your path that causes you not to be able to say truly: mihi vivere Christus est,12 for me, to live is Christ? Root out the obstacles which sometimes hinder your response! Today is a

Tell him that you can’t do it on your own, that he has to help you. Shout it aloud like the man stretched beside the pool whom we read about in the Gospel: hominem non habeo,13 I have no-one to help me. Tell St Joseph that. Today could be the day for a new conversion, a deeper conversion that is final and permanent. Hodie, si vocem eius audieritis, nolite obdurare corda vestra,14 if you hear his voice, harden not your hearts. You are listening to your Father, to the Founder of Opus Dei, and although that’s a lot (God will call you to account for having lived with the Founder, even though I’m a very poor specimen), it’s not just I who am speaking to you… God himself, in the depths of your heart, is asking you to open the doors wide so his grace can enter into the deepest recesses of your soul, to heal and purify and enlighten it. You and I cannot have a hard heart, like stony ground that the drops of water can’t penetrate. No. Let us ask our Lord to enter into us like life-giving rain, that makes the ground on which it falls bear rich fruit.

Humble sincerity I would like to remind you once more that we have come to the Work to become saints. That is the purpose of our divine calling. We shouldn’t be surprised, therefore, on seeing that we still have a long way to go. And so we should acknowledge our deficiencies in all simplicity, without trying to dress them up as virtue. We should avoid pride, which blinds us terribly and makes us see everything the wrong way around. My children, be sincere with yourselves, be objective. Like that we will be effective in our dedication. It’s difficult: we have to be humble, to open our heart wide in spiritual direction so as to ventilate all the recesses of the soul. My children, flee from the “dumb devil”! The devil takes our sense of shame away in order to cause us to stumble, and then he gives us back an even greater sense of shame, to induce us not to mention our mistakes – perhaps the same mistakes as other people are prepared to brag about, and exaggerate, over a cup of coffee. You shouldn’t behave like that. Reveal everything you have within that bothers you, both small things and great, and like that you will always be victorious. Don’t forget that the more we are in the wrong, the more arguments the devil will supply us with for keeping quiet and not being sincere. I know that you are doing your own prayer, while my mind, my heart and my lips are giving expression to the prayer of us all. Let us have recourse to our Father and Lord. For us Joseph, apart from these titles of Father and Lord, is also Teacher, because he teaches us, he instructs us and sets us an example; because, with his patronage, he gets us grace from heaven, helps us and makes us effective. Ask him for a refined sense of obedience. Ask him for a responsible attitude to work, and a love that is expressed in deeds. Ask him for

We have the good custom of turning to our Mother, to whom, every day, we take the little flowers of our resolutions, those humble and hidden violets we pick up during the day. Now, on your own, you should invoke Mary and invoke Joseph. See how our meditation today has given us a lot to think about, a lot to ask for, and a lot to put into practice.

In Joseph’s Workshop*

The whole Church recognises St Joseph as its protector and patron. Down the centuries he has been spoken about, emphasis has been given to various aspects of his life which was ever faithful to the mission God had entrusted to him. That is why, for many years now, I have liked to invoke him with an affectionate title: “our Father and Lord”. St Joseph really is “Father and Lord”, protecting and accompanying on their journey through this life those who venerate him, as he protected and accompanied Jesus while he was growing up to manhood. As one gets to know him, one discovers that the Holy Patriarch is also Master of the interior life: because he teaches us to get to know Jesus, to share our life with him, and to realise that we are part of God’s family. St Joseph teaches us these lessons, being, as he was, an ordinary man, father of a family, a worker who earned his living with the work of his hands. And this fact has also for us a significance which leads to reflection and is a motive for joy. As we celebrate his feast today, I wish to bring to mind what the Gospel says about him so as to be able to discover better what God is telling us through the simple life of Holy Mary’s Spouse.

St Joseph in the Gospel Both St Matthew and St Luke tell us that Joseph came from a noble line: the house of David and Solomon, kings of Israel. The details of his ancestry are somewhat confused from the historical point of view. We do not know which of the two genealogies, that the Gospel writers bring us, refers to Mary – Jesus’ Mother according to the flesh – and which to St Joseph, who was his father according to Jewish law. Nor do we know if Joseph’s birth place was Bethlehem, where he went to be enrolled, or Nazareth, where he lived and worked. Instead, we do know that he was not a rich person: he was a worker, like millions of other men all over the world. He worked at the tiring and humble occupation which God had chosen for himself, when he took our flesh and wished to live for thirty years as one of us. Sacred Scripture tells us St Joseph was a craftsman. Various Fathers add that he was a carpenter. St Justin, talking of the life of Jesus, says that he made ploughs and yokes.1 Perhaps basing himself on these words St Isidore of Seville concludes that Joseph was a blacksmith. In any event, a workman working at the service of his fellow citizens, who had a manual skill acquired through years of effort and sweat. From the Gospel narratives we come to see Joseph’s great human personality: at no moment does he appear as someone frightened or shy of life. On the contrary, he faces up to problems, deals with difficult situations and takes responsibility and initiative in the tasks entrusted to him.

I don’t agree with the classical portrayal of St Joseph as an old man, even though it may have been done with the good intention of emphasising the perpetual virginity of Mary. I imagine him young and strong, perhaps a few years older than our Lady, but in the prime of his life and human energy. You don’t have to wait to be old or lacking in vigour to practise the virtue of chastity. Purity is born of love, and the robustness and gaiety of youth are no obstacle for a clean love. Joseph was young in heart and body when he entered into marriage with Mary, when he learned of the mystery of her divine Motherhood, when he lived next to her respecting the integrity God wished to bestow upon the world as one more sign of his coming among creatures. Anyone who cannot understand a love like that knows very little of what true love is, and is entirely ignorant of the Christian meaning of chastity. Joseph was, we have said, a craftsman from Galilee, one man like so many others. And what does life have to offer to an inhabitant from a forgotten village, such as Nazareth? Only work, every day, with the same constant effort. And, at the end of the day, a poor little house, in which to regain energy and begin one’s work again the following day. But the name Joseph, in Hebrew, means “God will add.” God adds, to the holy lives of those who do his will, unsuspected dimensions: the important thing, what gives value to everything, the divine. God, to the humble and holy life of Joseph, added – if I may put it this way – the life of the Virgin Mary and that of Jesus, our Lord. God never lets himself be outdone in generosity. Joseph could make his own the words spoken by Mary, his spouse: Quia fecit mihi magna qui potens est, he who is mighty has done great things in me, quia respexit humilitatem, because he has looked upon my lowliness, my littleness.2 Joseph was an ordinary man, on whom God relied to do great things. He knew how to live, exactly as the Lord wanted, each and every event that went to make up his life. That is why Holy Scripture praises Joseph, affirming that he was “a just man”.3 And, in the Hebrew tongue, a just man means a devout and irreproachable servant of God, someone who fulfils the divine will;4 elsewhere it signifies being good and charitable towards one’s neighbour.5 In short, the just man is one who loves God and proves his love by keeping God’s commandments and directing his whole life towards the service of his brothers, his fellow men.

Joseph’s faith, love and hope Justice does not consist in mere submission to a rule: rectitude should grow from the inside; it should be deep and alive, because “the just man lives by faith”.6 To live by faith: we see these words, which were later so often a subject of meditation for the apostle Paul, put into practice most effectively by St Joseph. He did not fulfil the will of God in a routine or perfunctory way; he did it

For him the law by which every practising Jew lived was not a mere code nor a cold list of precepts, but an expression of the will of the living God. That was why he was able to recognise the Lord’s voice when it came to him unexpectedly, surprisingly. The life of the Holy Patriarch was simple, but it was not easy. After some moments of deep anxiety, he learns that the Son of Mary has been conceived through the Holy Spirit. And this Child, Son of God, descendant of David according to the flesh, is born in a cave. Angels celebrate his birth and distinguished people from distant countries come to adore him. But the King of Judea wants to kill him and flight becomes necessary. The Son of God is, to all appearances, a defenceless child who is to live in Egypt. When relating these events in his Gospel, St Matthew constantly highlights Joseph’s faithfulness. He carries out God’s commands without wavering, even though at times the meaning of those commands may have seemed obscure to him, or their connection with the rest of God’s plans was hidden from him. The Fathers of the Church and spiritual writers frequently underline this firmness of Joseph’s faith. Referring to the Angel’s command to flee from Herod and take refuge in Egypt,7 St John Chrysostom comments: “On hearing this, Joseph was not shocked nor did he say: ‘This is strange. You yourself made it known not long ago that he would save his people, and now he is incapable even of saving himself; instead we have to flee, to set out on a journey and endure a long displacement: this is contrary to your promise.’ Joseph does not think in this way, for he is a faithful man. Nor does he ask when to return, even though the Angel left it so vague, for he had said to him: ‘Stay there’ – in Egypt – ‘until until I tell you.’ Nevertheless, Joseph does not make difficulties for himself, but he obeys and believes and joyfully puts up with all the trials.” 8 Joseph’s faith does not falter; his obedience is strict and swift. To understand better the lesson the Holy Patriarch gives us here, it would be good to consider that his faith is active and that his docility is not that of one whose obedience lets him be carried away by the chain of events. Because Christian faith is the exact opposite of conformity, or of a lack of interior activity and energy. Joseph abandoned himself unreservedly into the hands of God, but he never refused to reflect on events, and so was able to obtain from the Lord that level of understanding of God’s works which is true wisdom. In this way he learned little by little that supernatural plans have a divine consistency, which at times is in contradiction with human plans. In the varied circumstances of his life, the Patriarch does not give up thinking, or neglect his responsibilities. On the contrary, he puts all his human experience at the service of the faith. When he returns from Egypt, “hearing that Archelaus reigned over Judea in place of his father Herod, he was afraid to go there.”9 He has learned to work within the divine plan and, in confirmation

7 Cf Mt 2:13 8 St John Chrysostom, In Matthaeum homiliae, 8, 3 (PG 57, 85) 9 Mt 2:22 9 that God does in fact want what Joseph has surmised, he receives an indication to retire to Galilee. That is the way St Joseph’s faith was: full, confident, complete, shown in an effective surrender to the will of God and an intelligent obedience. And, together with faith, charity, love. His faith fuses into Love: with the love of God, who was fulfilling the promises made to Abraham, Jacob and Moses; with the tenderness of a husband towards Mary, and with fatherly tenderness for Jesus. Faith and love in the hope of the great mission which God, making use too of him – a carpenter from Galilee – was beginning in the world: the redemption of men. Faith, love, hope: it is on these that Joseph’s life and all Christian lives depend. We see in Joseph’s life of self-giving an interweaving of faithful love, loving faith and confident hope. His feast day is thus a good moment for us all to renew our commitment to the Christian vocation, which the Lord has granted each one of us. When one sincerely desires to live on faith, love and hope, renewing one’s commitment is not a matter of taking up again something that had been in disuse. When there is faith, love and hope, to be renewed means – despite our personal errors, falls and weaknesses – keeping ourselves in God’s hands: confirming a path of faithfulness. Renewing our commitment means renewing, I repeat, our faithfulness to what the Lord wants of us: to love with deeds. Love necessarily has characteristic manifestations. Sometimes people speak of love as if it were an impulse of self-satisfaction, or a mere means for selfish fulfilment of one’s own personality. But it is not so: true love means going out of oneself, giving oneself. Love brings joy with it, but it is a joy which has its roots in the shape of a cross. As long as we are on earth and have not yet arrived at the fullness of the future life, there can be no true love without experiencing sacrifice and suffering: a suffering that tastes sweet, that is lovable, a source of intimate joy, but real suffering, for it entails conquering one’s own selfishness, and taking Love as the rule of each and every one of our actions. Works done for Love are always great, even though they may appear to be little things. God has drawn close to us men, poor creatures, and has told us that he loves us: Deliciae meae esse cum filiis hominum,10 my delights are to be among the sons of men. The Lord makes us realise that everything is important: those actions which, with human eyes, we regard as extraordinary; and those others which, instead, we rate as being of little account. Nothing is lost. No man is unappreciated by God. All of us, each of us following his own vocation – at home, in his profession or trade, in the fulfilment of the obligations that correspond to him because of his state in life, in his civic duties, in the exercise of his rights – are called to take part in the kingdom of heaven. St Joseph’s life teaches us this: it was simple, normal and ordinary, made up of years of work, ever the same work, of days monotonous from a human point of view, days following one day after another. I have often thought about this, meditating on St Joseph’s life, and it is one of the reasons which make me

10 Prov 8:31 10 feel a special devotion to him. When in his address at the closing of the first session of the Second Vatican Council, on 8 December last, the Holy Father John XXIII announced that the name of St Joseph was going to be mentioned in the canon of the Mass, a very important churchman phoned me straight away to tell me, “Rallegramenti! Congratulations! Listening to that announcement, I immediately thought of you and of how happy you’d be.” And indeed I was: because in the conciliar assembly, which represented the whole Church gathered together in the Holy Spirit, there was proclaimed the immense supernatural value of St Joseph’s life, the value of an ordinary life of work done before God, in total fulfilment of his divine will.

Sanctifying work, sanctifying oneself in work, sanctifying with work In describing the spirit of the association to which I have devoted my life, Opus Dei, I have said that it rests as though on its hinge upon ordinary work, on work done well carried out in the middle of the world. In calling us God gives us a mission, inviting us to share in the one and only task of the Church, so as to bear witness to Christ before our equals, our fellow men, and draw all things towards God. Vocation switches on a light that makes us recognise the meaning of our existence. Through it we come to see, with the radiance of faith, the reason for our life on earth. Our life, present, past and future, stands in fresh relief, with a depth we had not suspected before. All events and all the things that happen to us now find their proper place: we understand where the Lord wants to lead us, and we feel as if swept along by this task entrusted to us. God brings us out of the darkness of our ignorance, of our uncertain journeying through the eventualities of history and calls us with a strong voice as he once called Peter and Andrew: Venite post me, et faciam vos fieri piscatores hominum,11 follow me and I will make you fishers of men, whatever position we may occupy in the world. The man who lives by faith may meet with difficulty and struggle, suffering and even bitterness, but never discouragement or anguish because he knows that his life is worthwhile, he knows why he has come into this world. Ego sum lux mundi, Christ exclaimed, qui sequitur me non ambulat in tenebris, sed habebit lumen vitae.12 I am the light of the world; he who follows me does not walk in the darkness, but will possess the light of life. To deserve this light from God, we need to love, to be humble enough to recognise our need to be saved, and to say with Peter: “Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life; and we have believed, and have come to know, that you are the Christ, the Son of God.”13 If we really act in this way, if we allow God’s call to enter our heart, we, too, shall be able to repeat truthfully that we do not walk in darkness, for over and above our weaknesses and our personal defects the light of God will shine out, as the sun shines

11 Mt 4:19 12 Jn 8:12 13 Jn 6:68-69 11 above the storm. Christian faith and calling affect our whole existence, not just a part of it. Our relationship with God is necessarily a relationship of self-giving and embraces everything in us. The attitude of the person of faith is to look at life, with all its dimensions, from a new perspective: that which is given us by God. You, who celebrate with me today this feast of St Joseph, are all men dedicated to working in various human professions; you have your own homes, you belong to very different countries, races and tongues. You have been educated in lecture halls or in factories and offices. You have worked in your jobs for years, established professional and personal relationships with your colleagues, shared in solving the collective problems of your companies and the society in which you live. Well then: I remind you once again that all this is not foreign to God’s plans. Your human vocation is part, and an important part, of your divine vocation. That is the reason why you must sanctify yourselves, contributing at the same time to the sanctification of others, your fellow men, precisely by sanctifying your work and your environment: that profession or job which fills your days, which gives a particular character to your human personality, which is your way of being in the world; that home, that family of yours; and that nation, in which you were born and which you love. Work inevitably accompanies man’s life on earth. With it come effort, fatigue, tiredness: expressions of the suffering and struggle which form part of our present human existence, and which are signs of the reality of sin and the need for redemption. But in itself work is not a penalty or a curse or a punishment: those who speak of it in that way have not read Holy Scripture properly. It is time for us Christians to shout from the rooftops that work is a gift from God and that it makes no sense to divide men into different categories according to the type of work they do, considering some tasks nobler than others. Work, all work, bears witness to the dignity of man, to his dominion over creation. It is an opportunity to develop one’s personality. It is a bond of union with others, a fount of resources to provide for one’s family; a means to contribute to the improvement of the society in which one lives and to the progress of the entire human race. For a Christian these perspectives extend and grow wider. Because work comes to be seen as a participation in the creative work of God, who, when he created man, blessed him saying: “Be fruitful and multiply, and fill the earth and subdue it; and have dominion over the fish of the sea and over the birds of the air and over every living thing that moves upon the earth.”14 Because, moreover, since Christ took it upon himself, work has become for us something both redeemed and redemptive. Not only is it the context in which man lives, but a means and path to sanctity, something that can be sanctified and that can sanctify. It is well to remember, therefore, that this dignity of work is based on Love.

St Joseph, our Father and Lord*

We are celebrating the feast of St Joseph, our Father and Lord, protector and patron of the universal Church and of this family of God’s sons and daughters, Opus Dei. I suppose that you have sometimes asked yourselves: How is it possible for the devotion to St Joseph to have taken such deep root in the Work when it is a relatively recent devotion, having begun to flourish in the West only around the 16th century? I would reply that our affection, piety, and devotion for St Joseph is a consequence of our contemplative life. All of us in the Work try to be very close to Jesus and our Lady; and we cannot be on intimate terms with our Lord and his Mother – our Blessed Mother – unless we are very familiar also with the holy Patriarch who was head of the Family of Nazareth. Then, too, my children, the Church has rightly recommended him as Patron of the interior life. Who is there with more interior life than Joseph? What human being was closer to Jesus and Mary? Is there anyone more humble than Joseph, who goes completely unnoticed? A few days ago, while reading at Mass a passage from the book of Kings, a thought came to my mind and my heart about the simplicity our Lord asks of us in life – the very thing that characterized Joseph. When Naaman, the Syrian commander, finally goes to see the prophet Elisha in order to be cured of his leprosy, he is told to do something very simple: Go and wash in the Jordan seven times, and your flesh shall be restored, and you shall be clean.1 Naaman thinks arrogantly to himself: So the rivers in my country don’t have water as good as those in this land of Elisha? Have I come all the way from Damascus for this? I expected something striking, something extraordinary. But no! You are unclean; go and wash, says the prophet. And not just once, but repeatedly – seven times – a figure, it seems to me, of the sacraments. All this brought to mind the simple, hidden life of Joseph, who does nothing but ordinary things. St Joseph goes completely unnoticed. Sacred Scripture barely mentions him to us. But it does let us see him fulfilling the role of the head of a family. Hence if St Joseph is Patron of our interior life, if he is an incentive for our contemplative life, and if it is good for all God’s sons and daughters in Opus Dei to be close to him, then he also seems to me an excellent example for those who hold positions of government in the Work. Belonging to Joseph’s family*

Throughout my life, my dear children, I have always tried to pour into your hearts what God has given me. There is nothing in the spirit of Opus Dei that is not holy, because it is not the work of men but of divine Wisdom. Everything good that the Lord wished to place in your Father’s heart shines forth in its spirit. Anything bad you may see in my poor life is not the spirit of the Work but my defects. Pray for me, therefore, that I may be good and faithful. Among the gifts the Lord chose to give me is devotion to the Blessed Trinity: the Trinity of Heaven – Father, Son and Holy Spirit, one God – and the trinity on earth: Jesus, Mary and Joseph. I understand very well the unity and mutual love of the Holy Family. There were three hearts but only one love.

Fatherly solicitude I love St Joseph very much. I feel he is an extraordinary man. I have always imagined him as being young. That’s why I became upset when some bas-reliefs were made for the oratory of the Father showing him as a bearded old man. I immediately had someone paint his picture showing him as a young man full of strength and vitality. Some people can only imagine purity as something for old age. Yet old people cannot be chaste unless they have been chaste when they were young. It can easily happen that people who have not acquired purity in their youth end up in old age with some crudely unchaste habits. St Joseph must have been young when he married our Lady, who at that time had just passed puberty. Though young, he was pure, upright and chaste, and the reason was because he was in love. Only by filling the heart with love can we make sure that it will never rebel or go astray, but will remain faithful to God’s pure love. Last night, when I was already in bed, I invoked St Joseph many, many times in preparation for today’s feast. I saw very clearly that we really form part of his family. This is not a gratuitous statement. There are many reasons for saying so. First of all, because we are children of Mary his spouse, brothers of Jesus Christ and children of our Father in heaven. Then, because we form part of that family of which St Joseph has consented to be the head. That is why we have called him our Father and Lord since the beginning of the Work. Opus Dei has not found the going easy. Humanly speaking everything has been very difficult. I did not want ecclesiastical approvals that would have distorted our legal path, a path which did not exist then and which is still being forged now. Many people did not understand our juridical position. And it was even more difficult for them to understand our theological and ascetical features. There are still some who do not want to understand. Ours is a peaceful, pastoral wave that is covering the whole earth. I did not want ecclesiastical approvals of any kind; yet we had to work in many different places. Millions of souls awaited us!

We invoked St Joseph, who acted as our Lord’s father. Years passed. Until 1933 we could not begin the first corporate apostolate, the famous DYA academy. We gave classes in law and architecture,a hence the letters of the name, DYA, but what it really stood for was God and Daring.b That was what we needed to break the legal moulds, which is what we did, and to provide a new channel for the aspirations of the Christian soul that longed then and longs now to serve God wholeheartedly, with human limitations, but in the world, in ordinary professional work without being Religious or being equated to Religious. Several years passed before I wrote our first regulations. I recall that I had a pile of notes that I took from our experience. Ever since the 2nd of October 1928 the will of God had been very clear, but it was put into practice little by little, year by year. I avoided the pitfall of first making a suit and then forcing the child to fit inside. On the contrary, with those notes of experience I was taking measurements for what would be the right suit. After a few years had passed, one day I asked Don Alvaro and a couple of your older brothers to help me put all that material in order. That is the way we wrote the first regulations; in them there was no mention of vows or bows or boots or hoots,1 because it was not necessary then nor is it now.

The first Tabernacle In 1934, if I am not mistaken, we began the first student residence. At that time the atmosphere in my country was violently anticlerical. The authorities persecuted the Church, and Communism, the denial of all freedom, had got a foothold. We needed to have our Lord with us in the Tabernacle. Now it is an easy matter to set up a Tabernacle, but then it was very difficult. It was necessary to do many things; we had to be able to present a ‘sampler.’ Do you know what the sampler was? In the last century, when young ladies graduated from finishing school knowing a smattering of French and how to play the piano, more or less, they also had to do some needlework. They did some sewing, embroidery and darning. They added letters, numbers, birds – all kinds of things! They also put their name and the date. I have seen my grandmother Florencia’s sampler because my sister Carmen kept it. For finishing schools it was the equivalent of a degree. We had to do something like that so the Church would look upon us affectionately and allow us have the Blessed Sacrament in the house. From the depth of my heart I already felt this devotion to St Joseph that I have taught you. I thought of that other Joseph to whom the Egyptians had gone, following Pharaoh’s advice when they were hungering for good bread: Ite ad Ioseph,2 go to Joseph; he will give you wheat. I began to pray to St Joseph for the first Tabernacle. And so did the sons I then had at my side.

While we prayed for this intention, I tried to find the things we needed: vestments, tabernacle and so forth. We had no money. Whenever I got twenty-five pesetas together, which in those days was a fair amount of money, it had to go to meet some other more pressing need. So St Joseph is truly our Father and Lord because he has given us bread, the eucharistic bread, like a good father. Didn’t I just say that we belong to his family? In addition to providing us with spiritual food, we are united to him by invoking him before that get-together which is our prayer. Also, when we renew our dedication and when we join the Work definitively, St Joseph is present too. In the beginning I used to advance the date of people’s Fidelity because I needed you. I have never felt indispensable in any way. Some of you will recall my asking them: ‘If I die, do you promise before God to continue with the Work?’ I never thought that I was necessary because I’m not. Any one of you is better than me, and can be a very good instrument. At that time people did the Fidelity on the feast of St Joseph, associating the holy patriarch with the spiritual commitment to carry out the Work, convinced that it was the positive will of God.

With Joseph, to Mary and Jesus Besides, after our Lady, St Joseph is the person who was closest to Jesus on earth. I am very fond of the prayers the Church recommends priests to say as devotions before and after Mass. They recall that St Joseph cared for the Son of God just as our parents did for us. While we were getting dressed they would come and caress and hug us, smothering us so much with kisses that they sometimes hurt. Can you imagine how much St Joseph must have suffered when he discovered that Mary was expecting? He loved her dearly. He was aware of her inviolate purity. Only a revelation from God through an angel could put him at ease. He had already thought of a prudent solution. So as not to shame her, he would leave without saying anything. Yet how much it would hurt him to do so, seeing how much he loved her, heart and soul! And can you imagine his joy when he learned that the fruit of her womb was the work of the Holy Spirit? Love Jesus and his most holy Mother! About a year ago I received a very old and beautiful ivory statue of the Blessed Virgin Mary when she was with child. I was deeply touched. I am moved by the humility of God who wanted to be enclosed in Mary’s womb for the normal time, just like any other mortal; because he is perfectus Homo, perfect man, while at the same time perfectus Deus, perfect God, the Second Person of the most Blessed Trinity. Aren’t you moved by God’s humility? Aren’t you filled with love on seeing how God became man and did not seek any privilege? Like him, we do not want privileges either. We want to be plain, ordinary citizens like everyone else. How wonderful! We feel very much at ease in the home of Jesus, Mary and Joseph. They went unnoticed. Whenever I enter one of our oratories where there is a Tabernacle, I tell Jesus that I love him, and I invoke the Blessed Trinity. Then I give thanks to the Angels guarding the Tabernacle, adoring Christ in the Eucharist. Can’t you imagine how astonished the Angels must have been on seeing how the Lord emptied himself, wanting to seem a mere man in that house in Nazareth, and earlier still in Bethlehem, and during the flight into Egypt, and then after their return when they feared losing Jesus because the son of a cruel monarch was reigning? We will never love Jesus sufficiently if we don’t thank him with all our heart for having chosen to be perfectus Homo.

Prayer for the Church My children, if this were a meditation I would keep going, but it seems to me enough for a get-together. You have sufficient material for a period of contemplative prayer, each one on his own: living with Jesus, Mary and Joseph in that home and in that workshop at Nazareth; contemplating the death of the Holy Patriarch, who according to tradition was accompanied in his last moments by Jesus and Mary; and telling him that we love him very much, asking him never to forsake us. If it were possible to be sad in heaven, St Joseph would be very sad nowadays on seeing how the Church is decomposing as if it were a corpse. Yet the Church is not a corpse. Individuals will pass away, times will change, and blasphemies and heresies will be heard no more. Now they are spread abroad without hindrance because there are no shepherds to point out the wolf. Anyone who proclaims the truth runs the risk of being persecuted and slandered. The only people who seem to be able to act with impunity are those who propagate heresy and foster dissolute practices. The worst enemies are within and in high places. Don’t let yourselves be deceived. When you pick up a book on some religious topic, if you’re not sure that it is perfectly sound it should burn your fingers. Drop it at once! It’s like deadly poison. Get rid of it even faster than if it were pornography, because pornography at least can be seen, whereas this can filter through the smallest pores. Join me in invoking St Joseph with all your hearts to persuade the Blessed Trinity, and our Lady his Spouse, our Mother, to shorten the time of trial. On St Joseph’s Day*

Today is the solemnity of St. Joseph, and the whole Church celebrates the day of its holy Patron.– For us, faithful children of our Holy Mother, the Church, it is a day that is even more special – because, as our Founder used to say, “it is to this Family that we belong”... to the Holy Family of Jesus, Mary, and Joseph. We have often considered how Jesus, dying on the Cross, gave His Mother to us, so that she could be our Mother.1 And so we are His brothers and sisters, and by that very fact we have a right to live in the house of Nazareth, the home owned by St. Joseph, our Father and Lord. But, in addition to that, we in the Work belong to the Holy Family for another reason: our vocation to Opus Dei. Today we have already kept this in mind, and we have thanked Our Lord for it. How good our God is! He could have chosen other people, others perhaps more suitable than ourselves, and called them to the Work: But instead He turned to you and me, my son – to every one of us, out of His mercy alone, without any merit on our part. Moved by His grace, we answered “Yes”; and today, once more, we have renewed our dedication. Many of your brothers, throughout the world, even if they haven’t done the Oblation yet, have told Our Lord that they want to be faithful, because they know that our vocation requires an unconditional giving of ourselves, for good. Others have renewed the Oblation on St. Joseph’s day, as our statutes require; and many more, out of devotion, have renewed their Fidelity. We have all said words expressing our love: My God, I want to be faithful all my life; grant me your help, so that I may maintain this faithfulness to the very last moment. The Church is a good Mother, who gives us many spiritual gifts; years ago, we were granted an indulgence every time we renew, silently or with words, our_ dedication, as well as at the moment we make the Oblation or Fidelity. The Church takes these graces from the treasury of the merits of Christ, of our Lady, and of the saints. It has received this treasury in trust, and distributes it, so that we can become worthier and better instruments in God’s service. In this way, the sanctifying action of the Church is added to the direct action of the Holy Spirit on our souls. When our Lord calls anyone to his service, he grants him his grace in a way that is more effective and more constant. If we respond with docility, God’s action becomes even more intense, sanctifies us, and makes us worthy of his love. Before, we weren’t worthy – our Lord loved us because he wanted to. Then, if we respond – and that is always the result of grace – he enriches us and transforms us; we become divinized. And all the more so if we understand more fully that we are nothing, we have nothing, we can do nothing. The humbler we are, the closer we will be to God’s heart.

He requires us to respond – to love him in a real way, to give ourselves seriously...For this He offers us his love, the Holy Spirit, who pours himself out into our hearts, together with his grace and with all his supernatural virtues and gifts. How good He is! When we consider this reality, we realize that we ought to be driven mad with gratitude, and that our giving of ourselves should be much more generous. Think about it in your prayer – God gives Himself to us as He is: the Supreme Good, all-powerful, He who is Love itself... and we are so small, almost nothing,, In this exchange of love, we are the ones who come out ahead. But we must give the little that we are and have, like the poor widow who put two small coins into the Temple treasury.2 It was very little, but it was all she had to live on. Our Lord made him master and lord of his household. He was the patriarch of that home, and had all the authority that the head of a family used to have in those times. And yet, what did St. Joseph do in the house of Nazareth? He served Jesus and Mary and gave us an example of service. What great virtue is found in our Father and Lord, St. Joseph! He is the just and faithful man of the Gospel, praised in Sacred Scripture.4 He had to have extraordinary virtue to be able to govern that home.. He exercised the authority he had received while feeling ashamed of his unworthiness; in human things, he was the one who taught the Son of God. Joseph, who seemed to be the master of the house, was really the slave of Jesus and Mary.
