## INTRODUCTION {#cuaderno.12.0}

In 1946 our Father wrote a letter in which, with great foresight, he encouraged his daughters and sons of all times to make use of the means of social communication to place Christ at the summit of all earthly activities. He wanted to spur us on so that with a deep sense of humility – strong in the name of our God and not in the resources of our horses and chariots (cf Ps 19:8) – we might be present fearlessly in all human activities and organisations, so that Christ may be present in them.1 This new volume in the “Notebook” series contains many quotations from that letter, as well as other ideas from our Founder about the apostolate of public opinion. One of its principal messages is that this apostolate is a task which involves everyone. From all the professions, wrote our Father, one can come to exert an influence on the life of the organs of public opinion. The apostolic zeal of my children, on seeing the transcendence of this apostolate, will suggest to each one – in the sphere of their own initiatives – what way they can help.2 Each one of us, whatever our position in society, is called to cooperate in the great adventure of giving a Christian tone to the work and the message of the communications media. Some – the media professionals – will do so directly, as also will all those who make use of the media to transmit their knowledge and give doctrine in relation to their own particular field. Others, the majority, do so as ordinary citizens who use the media and can and ought to play an ever more active part in them.

Some of the chapters in this volume, like “The Word of God is not enchained” “The Truth will set you free” and “The precious gift of freedom”, speak about that dominant passion, so characteristic of the children of God in Opus Dei, which is to give doctrine. The greatest enemy God has in the world is ignorance,3 our Father used to say, and the Apostolate of public opinion is aimed precisely at that enemy, making use of weapons which, because of their characteristics, play a decisive role in the shaping of society. We shouldn’t forget that the media can have an immeasurable magnifying effect on the action of a single individual, in some cases on a global scale, and so this apostolate constitutes a splendid means of spreading the Christian faith. Giving doctrine is a process in which one has to scatter the seed with both hands, but it must be done skilfully and not in any old way. The sower has to be professionally competent, and should know what means to use, and the right time and place to make his work effective. And the same happens in the apostolate: giving doctrine is something one has to learn. On the one hand, we have to be able at any moment to feel the pulse of public opinion so we can identify its achievements and deficiencies and analyse their origins. Only in this way can we manage to heal and improve the social, professional and family environments and help society become more human and more Christian. On the other hand, we need to think about how best to express what we have to say, in keeping with the gift of tongues our Father spoke about. Our contributions should be deeply imbued with a Christian outlook and help our readers or viewers discover true and uplifting answers to their questions and problems. In searching for better forms of expression we should bear in mind that we live in a society which is conditioned by the “culture of the image”, in the sense that an example is normally more persuasive than a discourse, and facts more convincing than words. ## THE MEDIA {#cuaderno.12.1}

During a trip Don Alvaro made by car through Castile in 1989, the road several times crossed a river named “Sequillo” (the “dry one”). And it lived up to its name, he later remarked, since it was practically waterless. This observation brought to his mind our Father’s graphic description of a soul in love with God: it is like a river in flood that overflows its banks and fertilises the surrounding fields with its rich deposit. Don Alvaro concluded by saying that we cannot be like the Sequillo River. We have to reach every corner of the world, all souls, with the power of our love. Our Founder at times summed up our apostolic zeal in these words: Get to know Jesus Christ, make him known, bring him everywhere.1 As the Father reminded us in one of his letters: Jesus Christ! We have but to pronounce that holy name and our soul is filled with delight, with a deep and serene joy, which we want to spread around us, in concentric circles that grow ever wider, taking to the whole world the knowledge of Christ, who is the Way, the Truth and the Life (cf John 14:6).2

## FASHIONING PUBLIC OPINION {#cuaderno.12.2}

Our Father was always very clear about the great human and Christian service that can be done by those who work in the means of social communication. He wanted to see knowledge of Christ’s doctrine and life spread far and wide, and he knew what a great role the media could play in this task. Back in 1940, with the Spanish Civil War just ended, he agreed – at the request of a friend – to give a series of classes on Professional Ethics in the Official School of Journalism in Madrid. He spoke and wrote a lot about the social and apostolic responsibilities of Christians in this area. Immense horizons of apostolate were open to them, he explained. Our Father loved every human endeavour, was interested in every type of work, and wanted to see Christ reign over everything. This was what the early Christians had done. They were active at all the crossroads of culture and commerce, and in all those activities where people meet and exchange news and ideas. They were daring. They knew how to use and evaluate the goodness and truth of what they heard in order to spread the Gospel. In his preaching St Paul, the Apostle to the Gentiles, used many examples taken from military life, from social customs, from the law and from the philosophical wisdom of the time. In his discourse in the Areopagus in Athens – where the greatest thinkers of the day came together – he even quotes from a Greek poet.1 In the second century, St Justin Martyr affirmed that all the truths discovered by the wise men of the past belonged to

And he defended the faith with the gift of tongues, adapting his mode of expression to the mentality of his listeners. Nowadays, too, there are “Areopagi” like in ancient Athens. This is how St John Paul II has described today’s great centres of art, culture and communication; and the working environments where ideas are forged, where human rights are fostered, where the peace and progress of peoples are built up and the natural environment safeguarded. In many of these “Areopagi” the light of Christ does not shine and a great confusion reigns. In such darkness, it is difficult to walk without stumbling or losing one’s way, and to distinguish between truth and falsehood, between real good and what could be a threat. For Christians who work in such areas, their mission is to give light and flavour: You are the salt of the earth (...) You are the light of the world. A city set on a hill cannot be hid. Nor do men light a lamp and put it under a bushel, but on a stand, and it gives light to all in the house. Let your light so shine before men.3

Fashioning public opinion These “Areopagi” of today are able not only to influence society, but even to propose ideas and uniform models for behaviour which reach the whole world. Thanks to communications technology, nations of widely divergent cultures and history can share fashions, amusements and entertainment which are very similar. The same films are shown, the same songs are popular, and inevitably, many opinions and sources of information are shared. In times gone by it was inconceivable that the written, spoken and visual word could be transmitted so quickly. ## OUR CULTURAL HERITAGE {#cuaderno.12.9}

Since you want to acquire a Catholic or universal mentality, here are some characteristics you should aim at:

In a broad sense, culture stands for cultivation of the spirit, of the intellectual life. And this is carried out largely by studying what other people have learned and done. An uncultured intelligence is one that is uncultivated, anarchic and confused: events leave some impression on it, but in such a disordered and confused manner that it cannot know anything with certainty. The cultured person is one who has organised his knowledge and increased it with the help of that of others. ## TOLERANCE {#cuaderno.12.14}

When conflicts arise at different levels – in the family, between neighbours, or with the State – there is a tendency nowadays to insist on the practice of tolerance, which is sometimes proposed as the supreme remedy for all social strife. Not everyone, however, shares the same view of what tolerance means or about how it can be applied. And so it is appropriate to remind ourselves of the Church’s teaching on tolerance, as well as what she has to say about other even more fundamental values, without which it is difficult to understand tolerance properly and the role it plays in the resolution of conflicts. In moral terms, to tolerate signifies not preventing something which is considered wrong, but without approving it. One is said to tolerate an error or an evil when one has the power to prevent it but does not do so in order to avoid a greater evil.1 There are situations in which to tolerate an evil is permissible (as we shall see), and others in which it is not. In any event, love for the truth and love for other people are, especially for a Christian, pre-conditions for a rightful tolerance, which, as St John Paul II wrote, “is not a passive virtue, since it has its roots in an operative love, and tends to transform itself and become a positive effort to guarantee freedom and peace for everyone.”2 In the present climate, however, a different idea of tolerance has gained currency. ## GOD ALWAYS PREVAILS {#cuaderno.12.18}

For a son or daughter of God with a strong faith, nothing is impossible. How many times our Father experienced this in his own life! He knew prayer was all-powerful, as Christ himself taught us. And as a young priest he was witness to a very significant proof of this truth. He spoke about it in the third person, as with so many other autobiographical events. Between the years 1927 and 1931 there was a poor woman who used to go to a priest for spiritual guidance. She was mentally retarded, ignorant and uncultured, but she had an exquisitely refined soul.
