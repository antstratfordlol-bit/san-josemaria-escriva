## THE HUMAN FOUNDATION OF CHRISTIAN LIFE {#cuaderno.9.1}

Holiness, the fullness of Christian life, is the work of grace. But normally it is built on a mature personality, a resolute will and wellrooted habits. The kingdom of heaven has opened to force, and the forceful are even now making it their prize.1 The higher the level of a person’s human moral qualities, the easier it is to be in tune with the work of grace. According to traditional teaching,2 human maturity stems from the harmonious, easy and constant putting into action of habits that man can acquire and develop by his own efforts. They are therefore called natural or human virtues. While some practice of these virtues can be attained without supernatural grace, the disorder brought into human nature by original sin has placed human perfection out of man’s reach; this of course applies even more so to supernatural perfection: no one is saved without the grace of Christ.3 With grace, on the other hand, the soul is enriched by the theological virtues and the gifts of the Holy Spirit, which enable us to know and love God in a new way, and our very nature is healed and elevated. Man is then capable of rising above all created things and

Then, under the necessary help of divine grace, there will arise a generation of new men, the moulders of a new humanity.”5 Human virtues, then, turn out to be very important for those seeking Christian perfection, which requires both human and supernatural maturity. As St John Paul II recalled: “When we speak of virtues (...) we must always have in mind the real man, the actual man. Virtue is not something abstract, detached from life. On the contrary, it has deep ‘roots’ in life itself. It springs from the latter and forms it. Virtue has an impact on man’s life, on his actions and behaviour. It follows that, in all these reflections, we are speaking not so much of the virtue as of the man, who lives and acts ‘virtuously’; we are speaking of the prudent, 6 just and courageous man.”

St Thomas Aquinas, following on Aristotle’s definition, 4 concludes: “prudence is right reason applied to action.” We need prudence to attain the human and supernatural maturity to which God calls us. Consequently, in the work of formation, we have to teach our friends to be prudent, to be persons of sound judgement, who ponder, reflect, have a right criterion and are docile; and at the same time, persons who are decisive, who carry out a decision once they know what ought to be done.

How to be prudent “The prudent man, who strives for everything that is really good, endeavours to measure every thing, every situation and his whole activity according to the yardstick of moral good. “So a prudent man is not one who – as is often meant – is able to wangle things in life and draw the greatest profit from it; but one who is able to construct his whole life according to the voice of upright conscience and according to the requirements of sound morality. “So,” St John Paul II continues, “prudence is the key for the accomplishment of the fundamental task that each of us has received 5 from God. This task is the perfection of man himself.” Prudence is exercised through three main acts, which constitute, as it were, the three stages leading to a right decision.6 In the first place, we must know objective reality clearly, as well as the moral principles that guide conscience and in whose light we judge our actions. This knowledge is increased through study, reflection on past experiences and the counsel of prudent and well-formed persons. The second step is the practical judgement about the morality of the specific action one intends to carry out and about the most suitable means to achieve the end sought. Finally, the will has to push to carry out the action or, alternatively, to refrain from doing so. Prudence does not consist, therefore, in indefinitely weighing the pros and cons of a certain course of action without actually deciding anything. A prudent person, after studying a matter sufficiently, chooses what he considers the most appropriate solution and executes it without delay. Although each prudent decision is preceded by a number of steps, in practice it is a quick process, particularly in well-formed persons who

Without examining the matter for too long, at least in the more normal situations, the prudent person acts in most cases with connaturality, that is to say, thanks to a special familiarity with virtuous actions, towards which he has acquired a natural inclination by repeated actions. These three elements, knowledge, judgement and the will’s decision, are proper to prudence as an acquired virtue. To develop this virtue, Christians rely also on the help of grace, which heals nature from the wounds of sin. Also, with sanctifying grace, a Christian receives in his soul the infused virtue of prudence. A new kind of knowledge, the knowledge of faith, comes in here. So it may happen that something which from a human point of view is deemed imprudent turns out, in the supernatural life, to be extremely prudent: A truly prudent person is ever attentive to God’s promptings and, by his vigilant listening, receives in his soul the promise and reality of salvation: I glorify thee, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, for having hidden these things from the wise and prudent and revealed them to little ones (Mt 11:25).7

Nowadays, because these cases happen so often, and because of the violence of the attacks against faith and morals, it is especially important to ask for advice in everything that relates to reading (books and magazines, etc.), as well as shows and entertainment. ## LOVE FOR THE TRUTH {#cuaderno.9.6}

The Christian ideal does not rely on justice alone to regulate the relations of Christians with the rest of men, for justice and charity are seasoned with a collection of virtues that help us get along with others and deal with them in a friendly and trusting way. We need to cultivate in our daily lives virtues such as truthfulness, honesty, naturalness, sincerity, loyalty, gratitude, optimism, love of freedom. Only thus can we resemble the Master and live a fully Christian life. Iesus Christus, perfectus Deus, perfectus Homo – Jesus Christ, perfect God and perfect Man. There are many Christians who follow Christ and are astonished by his divinity, but forget him as Man…, and they fail in the practice of their supernatural virtues, despite all the external paraphernalia of piety, because they do nothing to acquire the human virtues.1

## A CHRISTIAN SOCIETY {#cuaderno.9.7}

In human relations, giving others their due, which is the object of the virtue of justice, cannot be reduced to the material; it is much more than the material conditions of life. ## TEMPERANCE AND SOBRIETY {#cuaderno.9.10}

While affirming the primacy of the spirit, Catholic doctrine has always also recognised the dignity of matter and of material beings. This dignity is based, on the one hand, on the goodness of God the Creator, who created all things out of nothing and was pleased with what he had made.1 On the other hand, when the Word became flesh he assumed human nature whole and entire. That was the way that Jesus Christ, true God and true man, carried out the redemption of the human race and the whole material universe. Therefore to put body and soul, matter and spirit in opposition, is not a Christian teaching. On the contrary, from the first centuries the Church has rejected both the materialisms which deny the spirit, and Manichean spiritualisms. The Church goes further and attributes a very special dignity to the human body bearing in mind the ultimate end to which God calls each person. St Paul confirms this when he writes: Do you not know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within you, which you have from God? You are not your own; you were bought with a price. Interior and external obstacles One of the most important characteristics of human maturity is its dynamic character. Whereas the full development of the body comes about in a natural way, with time, unless some external factor such as an illness intervenes, the maturity of the whole person, body and soul, is not usually attained if one is left to one’s own devices, if one is not directed, if one is not provided with care and formation to overcome obstacles and difficulties: and in the first place those posed by internal disorder – the tendency to disintegration of nature caused by original sin and by personal sins. Maturity in our affectivity: channelling our natural inclinations to the service of the whole person; giving the will its free and responsible guiding role, and facing up to the consequences that follow from our decisions.
