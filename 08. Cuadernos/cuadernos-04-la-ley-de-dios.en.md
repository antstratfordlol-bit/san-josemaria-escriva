## DIVINE WORSHIP AND REVERENCE — First Commandment (II) {#cuaderno.4.4}

The Catechism of St. Pius X teaches that *the first commandment is fulfilled by the practice of internal and external worship*,[^c04-1] by which we acknowledge, adore, love and serve God as our supreme Lord.[^c04-2] This is a fundamental human duty. God does not need the glory that we creatures are able to give him; but we, to the extent that we glorify Him by our worship, unite ourselves intimately to the one who is the infinite Good and the source of all goodness.

In order to render homage to God with our whole being and unite ourselves to Him, more than just interior adoration is required. *The human mind needs to be led to God by means of the sensible world...Hence, in divine worship the use of corporeal things is necessary so that by using signs, man's mind may be aroused to the spiritual acts which join him to God. Therefore, the internal acts of religion are principal and essential, while the exterior acts are secondary and subordinate to the internal ones.*[^c04-3]

Without a spirit of adoration, external cult would be soulless---sterile and empty. But united to it, our worship becomes a complete and total homage, attaining the measure of love required by the first of the commandments: *with all* one's heart, soul, mind and strength,[^c04-4]^\ ^giving oneself entirely.

For man to render cult to God with all his faculties, *sensible signs are the fitting mode of expression, because man derives his knowledge through sensible things. Therefore, natural reason dictates that man use sensible signs, offering them to God as a sign of due subjection and honor.*[^c04-5] **This is what we call sacrifice (an offering to God), which together with adoration and prayer constitutes what is essential in natural and revealed religion. By sacrifice we acknowledge God's lordship over all things, and at the same time the existence of sacred, divine realities, which we have to respect and venerate.

### Value of the sacred

When Moses, on Mount Horeb, heard God call to him from the burning bush, he also received this command: *Put off your shoes from your feet, for the place on which you are standing is holy ground*.[^c04-6] The presence of the Lord communicated a sacred character to that place, which one needed to respect as a sign of the veneration that the creature owes to its Creator and to everything in any way related to God's holiness.[^c04-7]

We know that God is the sovereign Lord of the whole universe. Christian doctrine tells us of his powerful presence in all created realities: *God not only protects and governs all things by his providence: also by his power, which is internal to each one, he impels to motion and action whatever moves and acts.*[^c04-8] **Contemplation of the marvelous structure of the world and its unfailing dynamism infuses, in a heart that is upright and well-disposed, a natural feeling of respect and delight before God and all his works, which bear an imprint of the divine perfection.

Nevertheless, everything that exists in the world has been put at the service of man, whom God willed to be the crown of his creation.[^c04-9] This is the secondary purpose of the material universe, subordinate to the supreme task of proclaiming the glory of God. The few goods that the Lord removed from man's dominion at the very beginning of history and reserved exclusively for himself act as a reminder of this ordination of ends, to prevent the immense power mankind has received from tarnishing this primary truth: only God is the absolute and true Lord.[^c04-10]^\ ^And thus the Lord said to Adam: *You may freely eat of every tree of the garden; but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat.*[^c04-11] **And later, when he established his Covenant with the people of Israel, he required the consecration of all first-born males,[^c04-12]^\ ^and the tithes and first-fruits.[^c04-13]

The existence of sacred realities that man cannot dispose of as he likes because they belong exclusively to God, is seen clearly in the revealed precepts that pertain to divine worship. God wanted to indicate in an express way that his cult should be carried out in sacred places expressly dedicated to this purpose, by sacred persons, the priests, and by means of ceremonies that were also sacred. A large part of the prescriptions that he communicated to Moses on Mt. Sinai were aimed at establishing strictly, even in the smallest details, the dignity of the things reserved for divine worship. Thus instructions are given for how the tabernacle was to be constructed, for the ark, the utensils, the altar, the vestments of the priests; how the victims to be offered had to be; what feasts should be celebrated; what tribe and persons were to exercise the priestly functions.[^c04-14]

All these indications show that sacred things are united in a special way to God's holiness. Thus the area of what is reserved or dedicated exclusively to God is marked out, in contrast to all else that is profane or secular. And this is the meaning of consecration, both of persons and of places or things, which is carried out by means of a special act exercised in God's name.[^c04-15]

In the Old Testament, with a divine pedagogy that prepared the path for the arrival of the one who was Holy and Just *par excellence*,[^c04-16]^\ ^God sought to infuse a profound respect for the sacred in his people, who were strongly tempted by pagan rites. Along with rewards, which encouraged splendor in worship, there were also great punishments.[^c04-17] Jesus Christ emphasized this teaching, in the spirit of the law of grace that he came to communicate to us. It was precisely zeal for the house of God,[^c04-18]^\ ^for its honor and glory, that constituted one of the signs of the Messiah. Christ made this manifest when he energetically ejected the merchants from the Temple.[^c04-19] And in his preaching he insisted on the respect owed to divine gifts: *Do not give dogs what is holy; and do not throw your pearls before swine*.[^c04-20]

Sacred realities are holy. Therefore they should be handled with particular veneration and care, as is demanded by divine Holiness. What makes something sacred is God's positive will, not a decision or act of men. ***Today some are trying to* *"**desacralize**"* *everything, making profane what by its very nature is sacred, including the priesthood itself. We want to bring to God all realities, each in accord with its nature and way of being: what God has made sacred, is truly sacred.***[^c04-21]

One of the most wide-spread errors is this regard is the attempt to suppress the distinction between the sacred and the profane, accentuating in a unilateral way the "sanctity" (rather than the goodness) of all created realities.[^c04-22]^\ ^At the same time, there is a lack of respect for what is connected with divine cult, even to the point of sacrilege. The loss of a sense of the sacred entails a grave forgetfulness of the rights of God. Some see in God only a useful concept for man's earthly life, and in religion only a human service that provides a vague bond of solidarity among men.

Desacralization is the result of a humanized religion, because it is impossible to respect things which in themselves---without the union with God that the sacred entails---are inferior to man. Our Father said: ***Anyone who doesn**'**t give importance to a simple bow of the head, not merely as sign of respect, but of love, doesn**'**t deserve to be called a Christian***.[^c04-23] It would be a sign that the divine---sacred---basis of cult has been lost, or at least weakened.

Desacralization leads, as an inevitable corollary, to a paganizing of man's life, to the loss of a sense of his supernatural vocation. One of the purposes of sacred realities, within the plan traced out by divine Wisdom, is to remind man of the radical demand of sanctity to which he is called: *Be holy, for I am holy.*[^c04-24] *You, therefore, must be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect*.[^c04-25] This vocation is made possible through the redemption worked by Christ. Upon founding his Church and instituting the sacraments, particularly the Holy Sacrifice of the Altar, he gave us not just types or symbols of sanctity, but things that are in themselves sacred, because they contain the divine grace that they signify; they truly cause it, and thereby effect a divinization of the person who receives them. ***Don**'**t you see that each sacrament is the love of God, with all its creative and redemptive power, giving itself to us by way of material means?***[^c04-26] Especially the Eucharist, where Jesus Christ himself is truly present, with his Body, Blood, Soul and Divinity. Whence, in this case, respect for the Sacrament becomes a true worship of *latria* or adoration, which is what man owes to God.

*Care for divine worship*

God's gifts have to be received with gratitude, with the care and reverence they deserve. As far as the sacraments are concerned, which by divine institution are the ordinary and necessary means to attain salvation,[^c04-27] not to receive them through culpable negligence or contempt can constitute a grave sin.[^c04-28] Also, in virtue of the power received from Jesus Christ, the Church has disposed that all Christians, when they have attained the use of reason, have to confess their mortal sins at least once a year, and receive communion at Easter and when they are in danger of death.[^c04-29]

The faculty that our Lord entrusted to his Church to establish and teach the worship pleasing to God is found not only in the liturgy, where *full public worship is performed by the Mystical Body of Jesus Christ, that is, by the Head and his members,*[^c04-30]^\ ^but also in watching over and orienting the private ways of venerating God, the Blessed Virgin, the angels and saints, ensuring that they are always in conformity with the rule of faith and true piety. To oppose these norms and foster a false or superfluous cult, unpleasing and offensive to God, would constitute a grave sin.[^c04-31]^\ ^On the other hand, secondary practices that detract from the dignity that should preside over all homage to God may not constitute a grave fault, because of the parvity of the matter or the ignorance, in good faith, of the one who falls into these errors.

It is sometimes said that one can sin against the virtue of religion "by excess." **But it would be a mistake[^c04-32] to conclude from this that one should eliminate every manifestation of dignity and splendor from worship and sacred objects. A Christian cannot see these signs of reverence for God, because they are costly, as an affront to persons who do not even have their basic material needs met. One who reasons in this way forgets that *the dignity of man rests above all on the fact that he is called to communion with God\....He cannot live fully according to truth unless he freely acknowledges that love and entrusts himself to his Creato*r*,*[^c04-33]^\ ^offering, like Abel,[^c04-34]^\ ^the best of his produce.

In the Work, the criterion is clear: ***We give God the best we can. By the attention and care that we put into divine worship**---**ordinarily carried out in small oratories**---**we will ensure that we never offer the sacrifice of Cain. As I have said before, when a man gives the woman he loves a sack of cement and three iron bars as a sign of affection, we will do the same for our Lord, who is in heaven and also in our tabernacles.***[^c04-35]

Properly speaking, there can be no excess in the worship of God. On the contrary, everything we do will always be little in comparison with God's infinite excellence and goodness. The moral norm here is as positive as the first commandment, from which it stems. The goal that we have to aim at is extreme refinement and generosity, striving never to be inconsiderate or miserly. *You shall not offer anything that has a blemish, for it will not be acceptable for you*.[^c04-36] ***That woman in the house of Simon the leper in Bethany, anointing the Master**'**s head with precious ointment, reminds us of the duty to be generous in the worship of God.***

*****All the richness, majesty and beauty possible would still seem too little to me.***

*****And against those who attack the richness of sacred vessels, of vestments and altars, we hear the praise given by Jesus,* *"**She has done me a good turn.**"***[^c04-37]

This elementary norm of homage and love for God includes ensuring that sacred objects have an appropriate richness and are treated ***with the greatest care and devotion:* sancta sancte tractanda! *These are God**'**s treasures.***[^c04-38] It also requires the strict and loving observance of the liturgical rubrics,[^c04-39] including due decorum in public and private practices of piety. ***Piety has its own good manners. Learn them. It**'**s a shame to see those* *"**pious**"* *people who don**'**t know how to attend Mass**---**even though they go daily**---**nor how to bless themselves (they make some weird gestures very hurriedly), nor how to bend the knee before the Tabernacle (their ridiculous genuflections seem a mockery), nor how to bow their heads reverently before an image of our Lady.***[^c04-40]

### Respect for sacred objects

The loss of a sense of the sacred leads first to irreverence and then to profanation: to the sin of sacrilege, a very grave offense against God.[^c04-41] The worst of all sacrileges is the unworthy confection, administration and reception of the sacraments; above all, of the Holy Eucharist, because it truly contains the very source of grace Himself.[^c04-42]

The sin of sacrilege consists in the deliberate and direct attack against the holiness of a sacred object,[^c04-43] precisely for the reason that it is considered sacred, that is, as belonging to God or as destined to his service. Sacrilege treats a sacred reality as though it were profane, and therefore for the exclusive use of man. It is not rare, then, that real sacrileges are committed based on the saying (which is true, if understood properly) that *the sacraments are for man.* It is true that the sacraments are at the service of the needs of souls, but it is also true that they were instituted by God and by no means left to human discretion. Thus they require certain conditions for validity and licitude,[^c04-44] which, if not complied with, make the sacrament invalid (it does not really exist), or result in its being confected or received illicitly. In both cases, if there was awareness and consent, the action is a sacrilege. For example, it is never licit to receive a sacrament "of the living" **(that is, a sacrament one must receive with one's soul "alive" **through sanctifying grace) in a state of mortal sin,[^c04-45] just as one cannot suppress or vary one of the essential elements of any sacrament, even to facilitate its reception by the faithful.

In addition to profaning the sacred, there is the danger of instrumentalizing it for temporal purposes, as happens, for example in the sin of simony.[^c04-46] The name comes from Simon Magus, in the Acts of the Apostles. When he saw that the Holy Spirit was given by the apostles' imposition of hands, he offered them money saying: *"Give me also this power, that any one on whom I lay my hands may receive the Holy Spirit." But Peter said to him, "Your silver perish with you, because you thought you could obtain the gift of God with money! You have neither part nor lot in this matter, for your heart is not right before God."*[^c04-47]^\ ^The heart of the person falling into simony denies one of the essential features of the supernatural, the absolutely free nature of God's gift, and tries to make use of it to obtain a temporal benefit. This betrayal reproduces in some way the selling of our Lord by Judas. Simony is always a very grave sin.[^c04-48]

Something similar happens when advantage is taken of the sacred condition of a person or thing to obtain temporal benefits, thus abusing the rights of God. When Jesus conferred a part of his powers on the apostles, he commanded them: *You received without pay, give without pay*.[^c04-49]^\ ^What theology refers to as graces *gratis datae,* which include the ordinary power of holy orders and of jurisdiction, as well as extraordinary charisms, are powers granted with a view to the salvation of souls. If they are used for a different purpose, their nature is perverted; they are profaned.[^c04-50]

## Respect for God

Both history and daily experience demonstrate that once one has chosen the path of indifference and irreverence towards sacred objects, one soon falls into a direct lack of respect for God, whether this is a matter of eluding, mocking or abusing divine providence, or of putting it to the test, testing the wisdom, goodness or power of God.[^c04-51]

At the root of tempting God lies a problem of faith, that is, doubting or being blind to God's perfections, which in itself is already a sin.[^c04-52] But in addition, when one takes the initiative in testing God, there is the malice of irreverence, for *no one presumes to tempt one of whose excellence he is sure*.[^c04-53] Therefore, expressly tempting God is a grave sin, one which is repeatedly condemned in Holy Scripture.[^c04-54]^\ ^Implicit temptation, on the other hand, is often a venial fault because of inadvertence or lightness of matter, or of ignorance which is only slightly culpable.

Another root of this kind of sin is presumption or unjustified hope of a special help that God has no reason to give, since ordinary means exist to solve those problems. Anyone who voluntarily and needlessly puts himself into a near occasion of sin, cannot expect the help of an extraordinary grace; just as one cannot demand a spectacular intervention of God to carry out a good work or save oneself from a danger. *When they persecute you in one town,* Jesus says*, flee to the next; for truly, I say to you, you will not have gone through all the towns of Israel, before the Son of man comes.*[^c04-55] **

Writing to the Romans, St. Paul speaks of those who *although they knew God they did not honor him as God or give thanks to him, but they became futile in their thinking and their senseless minds were darkened*.[^c04-56] This foolish blindness is shown in superstition, which is another sin against religion. ***They have no faith, but they do have superstitions. We laughed, and at the same time were sorry, when that tough character became alarmed at the sight of a black cat or at hearing a certain word which of itself meant nothing but for him was a bad omen.***[^c04-57]

Superstition is sometimes said to be sinning "by excess" **against religion. This is the word's etymology. But *the etymology of a word differs from its meaning.*[^c04-58] **In superstitious errors there is both an excess and a deficiency. There is an excess of concern about earthly goods which the person thoughtlessly fears losing. And there is a lack of religious sense, of respect for and confidence in God. The superstitious person does not accept the truth that *in everything God works for good with those who love him.*[^c04-59]^\ ^Instead of abandoning himself in God's hands, knowing that to the one who seeks *first his kingdom and his righteousness...all these things shall be yours as well*,[^c04-60] he acts like *the Gentiles* \[who anxiously\] *seek all these things*.[^c04-61]

Superstitious practices involve, then, a grave affront to God. By sinning against Christian faith and hope, they constitute an occasion of scandal and bad example, and contribute to spreading a pagan outlook on life. Their gravity can be subjectively mitigated by ignorance, especially among persons with little formation; but it is harder to find an excuse for the new superstitious beliefs that are spreading in apparently cultured sectors of society.

Also included here are the practices of divinization, which are often great frauds as well. In its original sense, divination[^c04-62] is an attempt to usurp a divine prerogative, and is frequently associated with idolatrous practices. As such, it is a grave sin expressly denounced by God.[^c04-63]^\ ^More recently, however, it has presented itself under the new aspect of a disordered and unhealthy cult of statistics, planning, etc., with a materialistic determinism that tries to *scientifically* plan man's future, denying his freedom, and consequently destroying the whole moral order. This is an old heresy condemned by the magisterium of the Church, because it is contrary to the truth to claim that human freedom is absolutely conditioned by astronomical, climatic, biological, or economic factors, etc. *According to the theory that the soul's movements are determined by the stars, there can be no such thing as personal accountability for one's actions and no way of judging what is right or wrong*.[^c04-64] The error is not in having foresight and, with ever more careful hypotheses and conjectures, preparing solutions for possible future problems. The evil is in losing sight of the fact that there is a natural limit to these provisions, and, above all, in setting them against divine Providence, instead of seeking to further it.

The confrontation of man against God is also seen in the sin of magic,[^c04-65] and in related practices such as occultism and spiritualism, degenerations that are typical of a paganized society. Magic presupposes a will unrestrained by God, a desire for power that excludes and even challenges God's laws. It seeks to eliminate any trace of God's glory and honor from both the sacred and the profane. It is also a sin that easily falls into invoking or dealing with the enemy of God, the devil.[^c04-66] Therefore it is the greatest perversion of the spirit of religion.

\* \* \* \* \*

It is instructive to consider this long chain of sins that takes its point of departure in irreverence towards things of worship and can end up in an alliance with the devil. There are no lack of examples among both peoples and individuals. Particularly in environments that were once Christians, these words of St. Peter are seen as true once more: *For if, after they have escaped the defilements of the world through the knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ, they are again entangled in them and overpowered, the last state has become worse for them than the first. For it would have been better for them never to have known the way of righteousness than after knowing it to turn back from the holy commandment delivered to them. It has happened to them according to the true proverb, The dog turns back to his own vomit, and the sow is washed only to wallow in the mire.*[^c04-67]

***Created and constituted as the crown and head of God**'**s physical creation, we have been ordained by nature to serve God and render him cult of adoration, love and praise*.**[^c04-68]^\ ^The first commandment of the Decalogue, which commands us to love God above all things, also requires us to render cult to him, to venerate everything related to divine worship. It also entails the conviction that God is near to us, that he watches over us with a wise and merciful Providence, to which we should abandon ourselves with the trust of children and the gratitude of one who knows that he is only going to receive good things.

*O come, let us sing to the Lord; let us make a joyful noise to the rock of our salvation! Let us come into his presence with thanksgiving; let us make a joyful noise to him with songs of praise! For the Lord is a great God, and a great King above all gods. In his hand are the depths of the earth; the heights of the mountains are his also. The sea is his, for he made it; for his hands formed the dry land. O come, let us worship and bow down, let us kneel before the Lord, our Maker! For he is our God, and we are the people of his pasture, and the sheep of his hand.*[^c04-69]

## SANCTIFYING FEAST DAYS — Third Commandment {#cuaderno.4.6}

*Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy.*[^c06-1] This precept closes the first part or tablet of the Decalogue, which comprises the norms directly regulating man's relations with God. This commandment also *follows logically from the preceding commandment as an outward manifestation of that interior reverence which was there enjoined. And since this act of worship cannot easily be performed in the midst of our ordinary occupations, a certain time for it, in keeping with its dignity, is here prescribed*.[^c06-2]

As with the other precepts of the Mosaic law, this divine commandment specifies a moral demand of human nature. *Just as nature requires that a certain time be reserved for each of our necessary bodily functions---for sleep, for nourishment, for recreation---so too must be met the natural demands of our souls, among which is that preeminent need for renewing our spirits which comes only in the contemplation of God*.[^c06-3] On the other hand, *his own social nature requires that man give external expression to these internal acts of religion, that he communicate with others on religious matters, and profess his religion in community.*[^c06-4] **Clearly, in order to fulfill this social duty of public cult, an authorized determination of the way and time that it should be carried out must be made. Otherwise, only individual and private worship would be possible.

Therefore from ancient times God wanted to specify this norm of natural morality through a revealed law, which would also indicate the days reserved for himself. Thus, by establishing a certain time-frame for worship, the use of time would also be consecrated to God, its Creator and Lord, who created *lights in the firmament of the heavens to separate the day from the night; and...for seasons and for days and years*.[^c06-5]

## The sabbatical precept in the Old Testament

In *Exodus* we are told that God established a special day of the week for the entire people to render worship to him.[^c06-6] This was to be a *day of rest, consecrated to Yahweh,* because the Lord himself *blessed the Sabbath day and sanctified it,* in such a way that man was to reserve it exclusively for God, abstaining from work,[^c06-7] offering oblations and sacrifices,[^c06-8] and rejoicing with peace of soul.[^c06-9]

In addition to reserving the Sabbath for himself, God instituted other feasts, in memory of the mercies and signs that he had carried out among his people. The principal memorials were: the flight from Egypt, on the feast of the Passover; the pilgrimage through the desert, on the feast of Tabernacles; and the promulgation of the law, on the solemnity of Pentecost.[^c06-10]

The sanctification of these days---their exclusive dedication to God---is seen as a serious duty that is emphasized repeatedly throughout the Old Testament, and imposed under threat of capital punishment,[^c06-11] whose strict application the Lord demanded[^c06-12] when it became necessary to impress upon the mind of the people of Israel the gravity of this precept. Later the prophets declared that one of the causes of God's anger was that his people had not kept the sabbath or celebrated the solemnities with true piety,[^c06-13] a sign that they did not respect the Lord or obey his commandments.

The moral meaning of this precept is expressed in Sacred Scripture: *You shall keep my Sabbaths, for this is a sign between me and you throughout your generations, that you may know that I, the Lord, sanctify you.*[^c06-14] The Sabbath was a symbol of the infinite Holiness of God, who had condescended to establish a pact with men. He promised them happiness in this life and in the life to come, but required that they adore him and recognize him as their only Lord.

Thus the Sabbath was *indeed a sign, pointing out to man that he was to consecrate himself to God and be holy, since even the very day is thus consecrated and made holy. Its holiness is therefore a sign of what man should be. Secondly, it was a sign and reminder that the whole of creation is the work of God. Thirdly, it was the sign to be forever remembered by the Jews of their liberation from the bondage of Egypt. God himself was explicit on this point. "You shall remember that you were a servant in the land of Egypt, and the Lord your God brought you out thence with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm; therefore the Lord your God has commanded you to keep the sabbath day."*[^c06-15] **

*Lastly, it was the sign of the great Sabbath to come, both spiritual and heavenly.*

*The spiritual Sabbath would be that holy and mysterious rest wherein the "old man," buried with Christ, rises to new life (*Rom *6: 6), and lives that life in a totally Christian way\...*

*This spiritual Sabbath is ultimately heavenly and eternal, for, as St. Cyril observes,*[^c06-16] *"there remains indeed a sabbath for the people of God,*[^c06-17] *namely that life in which we shall enjoy with Christ the possession of all good. For sin will have been destroyed."*[^c06-18]

The Sabbath precept then is intimately tied to worship;[^c06-19] the repose from productive activities has as its aim adoring God. In addition, since it takes place on the last day of the week, it points to the end to which every work day should be oriented. It is also a renewal and confirmation of faith and hope in the Messiah, who is to inaugurate the definitive rest in God.[^c06-20]

In summary, the precept of keeping holy the Sabbath is a requirement of the natural law. God specified that it was to be on the seventh day of each week, indicating, at the same time, the way of sanctifying it. The moral essence of this commandment---the consecration of time to God---is immutable, as immutable as the eternal law in which the natural law participates. But the specific indications that the Lord gave to the Israelites---*because it would have been a risk to leave a rustic people to their own devices in this matter*[^c06-21]---belong to the ceremonial part of the Mosaic law, which would be abrogated by the law of Christ.

*Sunday, the Lord's day*

One of the fundamental characteristics of Jesus' preaching is the absolute character of his commandments, which demand a total and unconditional adhesion: *with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind, and with all your strength.*[^c06-22] **Such a demand cannot be satisfied by an external, merely formal observance. This is why he clashed with the mentality of the Pharisees, and had numerous confrontations in regard to the Sabbath.[^c06-23] At no time did he diminish the holiness of that day,[^c06-24] but he would not consent to confusing the dedication of the Sabbath to God with human traditions. As *lord of the Sabbath*[^c06-25] **Jesus gave the correct interpretation to the divine command: *It is lawful to do good on the sabbath*,[^c06-26] to alleviate needs and cure illnesses of soul and body. Even more so, when the spiritual and corporal works of mercy spring from the same charity, the same filial love, that moves *the true worshipers \[to\] worship the Father in spirit and truth*.[^c06-27]

Just as the revelation of the New Testament is more perfect than that of the Old, so is the worship demanded by Christ, private as well as public, much more excellent. Since the time of our Redeemer, we are no longer dealing with figures and types, but with their supernatural reality. The ceremonial laws that specified the Third Commandment of the Decalogue in the Mosaic law, give way to those which the law of the Gospel requires. A new form of cult arises, because we have a new Priest and a new Victim who is offered, Jesus Christ, whose sacrificial action is perpetuated to the end of time in the holy sacrifice of the Altar. Just as the levitical priesthood and its offerings were shadows and figures of the new Priest and Victim,[^c06-28] so the old form of worship was a shadow and figure of the new worship, inaugurated and fulfilled by our Savior.[^c06-29] The Catholic liturgy is thus the *exercise of the priestly office of Jesus Christ*.[^c06-30]

Therefore *the specific obligation of the Sabbath was destined to lapse---along with all the other obligations of Jewish ceremonial worship---at the moment of Christ's death. For these ceremonies were but shadowy images prefiguring the true light which is Jesus Christ, and their whole reason for being ceased, therefore, with his coming.*[^c06-31]

Saturday lost its primacy when the reality it symbolized was accomplished: when Jesus, by his death and resurrection, freed us from the slavery of sin and the devil,[^c06-32] opening up for us a new life. The Son of God incarnate achieved his glorification, thus resting from his redemptive work in time---as the Trinity rested and was glorified upon the conclusion of the six days of creation---and calling all mankind to participate in his rest. Sunday is also the day that the Holy Spirit was sent to us, who by his gifts guides the souls that respond docilely, and lets them taste in advance here on earth the praise of God and joy that will be perpetual in heaven.[^c06-33]

With the old Sabbath precept no longer in effect, a new decision was needed to satisfy God's commandment. *The Apostles decided that of the seven days of the week the first should be consecrated as "the Lord's day."*[^c06-34] *By a tradition handed down from the apostles, which took its origin from the very day of Christ's resurrection, the Church celebrates the paschal mystery every seventh day, which day is appropriately called the Lord's day or Sunday\...The Lord's day is the original feast day, and it should be proposed to the faithful and taught to them so that it may become in fact a day of joy and of freedom from work.*[^c06-35]

Because of its apostolic foundation and direct connection with the day on which our Lord rose from the dead, it is clear that the Sunday precept is not merely a specification of a norm of the natural law that a human authority, whether ecclesiastical or civil, can decide to change. In contrast to purely ecclesiastical norms, which the authority of the Church can change and even abolish, and in contrast to the precepts that come immediately from God, and which therefore are removed from that authority, the commandment concerning Sunday falls outside the power of that authority because of its origin, since by divine will the reality prefigured by the old Sabbath commandment was to be carried out perpetually in the new law. When the apostles established that Sunday was to be kept holy, and precisely that day and no other, they were following what was unequivocally the will of God.

Therefore, for a Christian, Sunday is not a day like any other. All days have to be sanctified, since we have to seek God at all times and in all places; but our Lord has marked this day out as his own, *dies Dominicus*, the Lord's day, and a Christian has to sanctify it as God wishes, obeying the sovereign will of his Father and Creator.

What is specific about the Sunday precept is not the general consecration of one's time to God. That is already included in the first commandment. The specific requirement is to reserve a particular day for the praise and service of God, as he wishes to be praised and served. *It is the right of God, and within His power, to order that man put aside a day each week for proper and due worship of the divinity. He should direct his mind to heavenly things, setting aside daily business. He should explore the depths of his conscience in order to know how necessary and inviolable are his relations with God*.[^c06-36]

*Christian meaning of feast days*

*From the earliest times of the Church, and periodically thereafter, the Apostles and their successors instituted other days besides Sunday as special occasions for remembrance and thankful worship. Among these were, first of all, the great feast days commemorating the mysteries of our redemption; then the festivals in honor of the Blessed Virgin Mary, the Apostles and martyrs, and all the blessed now reigning with Christ.*[^c06-37] **

All of these feasts, together with the Sundays---distributed according to the seasons---constitute the liturgical year, which *is not a cold and lifeless representation of the events of the past, or a simple and bare record of a former age. It is rather Christ Himself who is ever living in His Church. Here He continues that journey of immense mercy which He lovingly began in His mortal life, going about doing good*[^c06-38] *with the design of bringing men to know His mysteries and in some way live by them*.[^c06-39] Hence the important role that the liturgical year holds in Christian life.

Upon instituting the feast of Christ the King, Pope Pius XI recalled that *people are instructed in the truths of faith and brought to appreciate the inner joys of religion far more effectively by the annual celebration of our sacred mysteries than by any official pronouncements of the teaching of the Church. Such pronouncements usually reach only a few and the more learned among the faithful; feasts reach them all. The former speak but once, the latter speak every year---in fact forever. The Church's teaching affects the mind primarily; her feasts affect both mind and heart, and have a salutary effect upon the whole of man's nature.*[^c06-40]

The liturgical feast days have arisen in the Church through the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, often at moments when it was important to reaffirm the adhesion of the faithful to some revealed dogma. Therefore they have an important instructive role. But also in this area there are some who are trying to eliminate, reduce and simplify. And we have the paradox that ***even while some people are trying to cut back on religious ceremonies, the state keeps introducing new and more complicated ones in society, the military, the university, etc. For people need these signs that are visible to the senses. Yet this is not our primary reason: rather, we want to show our love for God, to render worship to God, offered in Christ by men, who are made up of body and soul.***[^c06-41]

## Sanctification of feast days

The Catechism of Pius X teaches that *the Third Commandment, "remember to keep Sundays and holy days," commands us to honor God on these days by acts of external worship*[^c06-42] **and *forbids us to do "servile work" on Sundays and holy days*.[^c06-43]

The purpose of the precept is not only to have time needed to rest from work, or to provide time and suitable conditions for other activities of a cultural or recreational type, or to create an atmosphere in which it is easier to relax, or to dedicate more time to one's family. All of these are legitimate demands that the divine precept takes into consideration and fosters. But *the proper meaning of this first part of the commandment can be summarized as follows: we must take special care to set apart a certain fixed time, when, freed from bodily labor and worldly affairs, we may devote our whole being, soul and body, to the worship of God.*[^c06-44] **Or using other words, *two things are to be observed in the hallowing of the Sabbath. One of these is the end: and this is that man occupy himself with divine realities\...The other is cessation from work*,[^c06-45] **and all the advantages that this brings with it, which therefore have the nature of means.

In relation to the second aspect, the Christian precept is less strict than the requirements of the Old Testament: *in the New Law the observance of the Lord's day took the place of the observance of the Sabbath, not by virtue of the precept but by the institution of the Church and the custom of Christian people. For this observance is not figurative, as was the observance of the Sabbath in the Old Law. Hence the prohibition to work on the Lord's day is not so strict as on the Sabbath.*[^c06-46] **The Church only prohibits work *which requires labor of body rather than labor of mind,*[^c06-47] **dispensing the faithful from this prohibition whenever a proportionately grave reason exists.[^c06-48] In spite of this, the precept of Sunday rest is a grave obligation, to such an extent that *to perform unnecessary servile work on Sunday for a notable time can be sinful*.[^c06-49]

The Church has always urged the observance of this duty, defending it against the abuses of a *technological mentality that deprives Sunday of its unique dignity as a day of divine worship and rest for both individuals and families. This mentality treats Sunday just the same as any other day of the week, as far as rest and relaxation go As a matter of fact, members of the same family might be assigned to different days of rest depending upon the production and distribution schemes of that society.*[^c06-50] **In reference to rest on feast days, the Church's magisterium reminds us that it is not licit for anyone to abandon or require that others abandon the obligations that man has in regard to God or to himself.[^c06-51]

Rest *from labor is not to be understood as mere idleness\...but it should be rest from labor consecrated to God*.[^c06-52] Therefore, there is an even greater obligation to refrain from sin and idleness, for *it is written in the Book of Ecclesiasticus:* [^c06-53] *"Idleness teaches much evil". St. Jerome wrote to Rusticus: "Work without ceasing in some honorable task so that the devil will always find you occupied." Honor only the most important feasts. If you take too many days off, you will become lazy*.[^c06-54] It is not a matter, then, of not doing anything but, especially on feast days, ***having one**'**s whole day taken up with a flexible schedule in which, besides the daily norms of piety, an important place should be given to rest, which we all need, to family get-togethers, to reading, and to time set aside for an artistic or literary hobby or any other worthwhile pastime\...by filling the hours of the day usefully, doing everything as well as we can, and living little details of order, punctuality, and good humor***.[^c06-55]

In urging the fulfillment of the Sunday precept to rest, the Church is also trying to encourage family life: *It is right and necessary for man to cease for a time from labor, not merely to relax his body from daily hard work and likewise to refresh himself with decent recreation, but also to foster family unity, for this requires that all its members preserve a community of life and peaceful harmony.*[^c06-56] **Thus in the spirit of the precept, if not in its letter, we find included the duty to dedicate greater attention to one's family. This could be considered the principal duty, after that of worshipping God.

## The precept to attend Mass

According to Sacred Scripture, every feast in God's honor should culminate with the offering of a sacrifice. In the new economy of grace, in order to perpetuate his sacrifice on Calvary, Christ instituted the holy sacrifice of the Mass, and expressly ordered his disciples: *Do this in remembrance of me.*[^c06-57] **Thus, from apostolic times, liturgical gatherings have had the Eucharist as their center.[^c06-58] United to this tradition is the ecclesiastical precept, confirmed by the magisterium of the Church without interruption since the fourth century and present in canonical legislation, to attend Mass on holy days of obligation, among which are included each and every Sunday.[^c06-59] The norm of divine law that commands participation in our Lord's Sacrifice has thus been specified by the Church's commandment to attend Mass on the days of precept.[^c06-60]

This is a precept which, both because of its matter and of the way it is set forth, obliges *sub gravi*, unless it is impossible physically or morally to fulfill it, that is to say, given reasons proportional to the nature of the precept. In addition, these excusing reasons have to be greater if they lead to the more frequent omission of this duty.[^c06-61]

The Church's commandment is fulfilled only by participation in the sacrifice of the Mass. No other celebration, even if liturgical, satisfies the purpose of the divine precept. Therefore the legitimate authority may dispense someone in certain specific, always contingent, circumstances from the duty to attend Mass, but it does not have the power to commute it in the strict sense by some other pious practice. Anyone who finds it impossible to be present at the holy sacrifice of the Altar may (and should be encouraged to do so) praise God in some other way, but he does not thereby satisfy the obligation of the commandment.[^c06-62] Neither would a simple gathering of the faithful united to praise God, to listen to a homily, etc., without the holy Sacrifice, fulfill the requirements for perfect worship as established by God, and which is carried out by his sacred ministers who act *in persona Christi*.

On the other hand, it is precisely attendance at Mass, legitimately celebrated, that enables every Christian to take part in the worship owed to God. And this reason takes precedence to such an extent over any pastoral motive---such as gathering together the faithful who live in the same place, imparting religious instruction, etc.---that attendance at Mass should never be subordinated to these other considerations, nor conditioned by them in any way, because that conditioning might easily alter the very nature of the precept and thus diminish its purpose in the eyes of the faithful.

The commandment to attend Sunday Mass is grounded, it needs to be insisted, on two realities of divine origin: the sanctity of Sunday as the day reserved for our Lord, and the fullness of worship that is carried out in the holy sacrifice of the altar, *the source and summit of the Christian life.*[^c06-63]

***It is the sacrifice of Christ, offered to the Father with the cooperation of the Holy Spirit**---**an offering of infinite value, which perpetuates the work of the redemption in us and surpasses the sacrifices of the old law.***

***The holy Mass brings us face to face with one of the central mysteries of our faith, because it is the gift of the Blessed Trinity to the Church. It is because of this that we can consider the Mass as the center and the source of a Christian**'**s spiritual life.***[^c06-64]

In our apostolic work, it is important to teach people to sanctify feast days, by strengthening their life of piety, by practicing the works of mercy, by putting greater effort into family life, by dedicating some time to their own cultural formation, or to other activities of recreation or relaxation. But it is even more necessary to insist on the need to fulfill the Sunday precept.

Recently many false arguments have become widespread aimed at adulterating the holy status of this commandment. All of them stem, however, from the same root: anyone ***who fails to love the Mass fails to love Christ. We must make an effort to live the Mass with calm and serenity, with devotion and affection. Those who love acquire a finesse, a sensitivity of soul that makes them notice details that are sometimes very small, but that are important because they express the love of a passionate heart. This is how we should attend the holy Mass. And this is why I have always suspected that those who want the Mass to be over quickly show, with this insensitive attitude, that they have not yet realized what the sacrifice of the altar means.***[^c06-65]

Pope Pius XII exhorted the faithful to *return Sunday to its status of being a day of the Lord, a day of adoration and glorification of God in the holy Sacrifice, a day of prayer, of rest, of recollection and reflection, of intimate family life.* And referring to the materialistic campaign to remove **the "divine character" **from Sundays, **he said that *undoubtedly, the outcome of the struggle between the faith and disbelief will depend to a large extent on what happens to Sunday\...Here then is a broad field of action waiting for your efforts. Throw yourselves heroically into this work. Help us to give Sunday back to God, to Christ, to the Church, to the peace and happiness of families*.[^c06-66]

## LOVE FOR ONE'S PARENTS — Fourth Commandment (I) {#cuaderno.4.7}

The commandment to sanctify feast days concludes the first part of the Decalogue, which contains the precepts that *look directly at the goal, God Himself*.[^c07-1] Another seven commandments follow that *reach God only indirectly, i.e., through our neighbor, whom we are commanded to love because of God*.[^c07-2] **

Like the first three precepts, those of the second tablet are to be observed because God has established and confirmed them through revelation,[^c07-3] and because they form part of the natural law imprinted on man's heart by his Creator. These moral demands stemming from man's social nature are reflected in the bonds and commitments that each person has and contracts with his fellow men. They constitute an ensemble of rights and duties that have been elevated also to the supernatural order. For *having been created in the image of God, who "from one man has created the whole human race and made them live all over the face of the earth,"*[^c07-4] *all men are called to one and the same goal, namely God Himself*.[^c07-5]

Just as honoring God's holy name and sanctifying his feast days are consequences of the first commandment, so the precepts that prescribe love of one's neighbor proclaim the love of God, *for he who does not love his brother whom he has seen, cannot love God whom he has not seen.*[^c07-6] **This is especially true of the affection and respect we owe to our parents, for certainly *to our parents, of all our neighbors, we are under the greatest obligation. Hence, immediately after the precepts directing us to God, a place is given to the precept directing us to our parents, who are the specific principle of our being, just as God is the universal principle.*[^c07-7]

It is fitting then that the second part of the Decalogue opens with this admonition: *Honor your father and your mother, that your days may be long in the land which the Lord your God gives you.*[^c07-8] Jesus reminds us that this is a divine mandate, which no human tradition can abrogate.[^c07-9] And the apostles, faithful to the Gospel received from the Master, will urge with St. Paul: *Children, obey your parents in everything, for this pleases the Lord*.[^c07-10]

## Love of neighbor, universal and ordered

The commandment of charity, on which *depend all the law and the prophets*,[^c07-11] has a second part: *You shall love your neighbor as yourself.*[^c07-12] Our Lord extends this precept to all men and women, without any exceptions. He went so far as to say: *Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you, so that you may be sons of your Father who is in heaven; for he makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good, and sends rain on the just and on the unjust.*[^c07-13]

Christian love of neighbor is a reflection of God's charity, which embraces everyone: those who enjoy God's friendship and those who, being more distant, are invited to repentance. Therefore, if at times someone said: ***to love God above all things is easy, but to love one**'**s neighbor, both friends and enemies\...that is very difficult!***, ******our Father would answer energetically: ***If you really love God* ex toto corde tuo, et ex tota anima tua, et ex tota fortitudine tua *(Deut 6: 5), with your whole heart, your whole soul, and with all your strength, the love for your neighbor that you find so difficult would be a consequence of your first Love. And you would not feel enmity towards anyone.***[^c07-14]

The Love of God, affective and effective, demands ***an immense, unrestricted love that is shown in deeds of service (cf. 1 Jn 3: 18) towards all men and women of every nation, language, race or creed\...because Jesus Christ died for everyone, so that all might come to be children of God and our brothers.***[^c07-15]

Divine love is not only the source and root of love for our neighbor but also, by direct indication of our Lord, its measure: *A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another; even as I have loved you*.[^c07-16] Therefore a Christian must nourish in his heart the very same sentiments as Jesus Christ.[^c07-17] **This moral demand opens unlimited and completely new horizons to charity, at the same time as it makes clear that love for God has an absolute priority over all other loves, as Jesus himself recalled in his answer to his Mother, filled with delicacy and clarity: *Did you not know that I must be about my Father's business?*[^c07-18]

The fourth commandment, in reminding us of our obligation to honor those who gave us life, spurs us to live an *ordered* love for all souls, beginning with those with whom we are united by bonds as strong as filiation and fraternity.

Someone could think that the order of charity holds only for material works or benefits which, since they are limited, logically have to be used first for those who are close to us rather than for strangers. While as far as one's affections are concerned, we should love everyone equally, those close to us and strangers, friends and enemies alike. But St. Thomas points out that this opinion is unfounded. Though apparently magnanimous, it overlooks the unity between internal love and external works of charity. The true demonstrations of affection, its gifts and services, tend in themselves to be a sign of the intensity of one's love.[^c07-19]

We can understand the order of charity still more clearly if we consider that *love can be called greater or less in two ways. In one way, from the standpoint of the object; in another, from the intensity of the act. For to love someone is to will good to him. Accordingly, one can love one person more than another, either because he wills him a greater good, which is the object of love, or because he more intensely wills a good, i.e., with a more intense love. Therefore, with respect to the first, we ought to wish the good of eternal life to everyone; but with respect to the second, it is not necessary that we love all equally; since the intensity of an act results from the principle of the action, and the principle of the action is union and similarity, we ought to love in a higher degree and more intensely those who are more like us and more closely united to us.*[^c07-20]

***As theology teaches us, love is ordered. Somebody who does not love his mother and says he loves others is mistaken. Love everyone, but with order, and in such a way that even those whom you have little obligation to love feel happy, since they see that you love them***.[^c07-21]

A person who fails to observe the order of charity will be unable to love with God's love. His love will be a self-interested one, especially when a hypothetical love for those who are more distant leads one to mistreat those with whom one is united by bonds of human or supernatural fraternity. *As we have opportunity*, writes St. Paul, *let us do good to all men, and especially to those who are of the household of faith*.[^c07-22]

At the origin of this practical error one frequently finds a heretical assumption that identifies love for God with a generic love for all men and women, eliminating all order and hierarchy. Our Lord, responding to a Pharisee's question, taught that the greatest and first of the commandments of the Law is: *You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind\...And a second is like it, You shall love your neighbor as yourself.*[^c07-23] **St. Thomas points out that, by the word "second," *our Lord wanted to show that there is a hierarchy to his commandments\...Why then did he say that the second commandment is similar to the first? Because when we love another person, who is created in the image of God, we are loving God within that person. This is how love for God relates to love for neighbor*.[^c07-24]

It is within this framework that the commandments of the second tablet are to be observed.[^c07-25] The Catechism of the Council of Trent teaches *that God alone is to be loved for his own sake, and that our neighbor is to be loved by a love that both comes from God as its source and returns to him as its ultimate object. Therefore, if we love our parents, obey our superiors and respect our elders, it should be because God has made them and put them over us as his instruments whereby he protects and directs us*.[^c07-26] *If we honor them, we are really honoring not men but God. This is the true sense of the Fourth Commandment. This meaning is confirmed by our Lord's words, as recorded by St. Matthew*[^c07-27]*: "He who receives you receives me."*[^c07-28]

Since love for our fellow men is a consequence of love for God, and by its very nature subordinate to it, one can understand why anyone who equates love for God with love for neighbor commits a grievous mistake.[^c07-29] Jesus taught unequivocally: *He who loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me; and he who loves son or daughter more than me is not worthy of me.*[^c07-30] **And in even stronger words he said: *If any one comes to me and does not hate his own father and mother and wife and children and brothers and sisters, yes, and even his own life, he cannot be my disciple.*[^c07-31]

Love for God knows no limits. It can and should always grow; we will never reach the point of loving him as he deserves to be loved. Love for others, and first of all, love for our parents, has its origin and its measure in love for God. Therefore *if at any time the commands of their parents contradict those of God, there is no doubt that children should put the will of God before the will of their parents, according to the divine norm: "We must obey God rather than men."*[^c07-32]^-^[^c07-33]

### The most sweet precept

***The commandment to love our parents forms part of the natural law as well as the divine law, and we call it* the most sweet precept**.[^c07-34] Among other reasons, because a person with the spirit of Opus Dei grounds his Christian life on divine filiation, and therefore finds it very pleasing and lovable to fulfill his duties of natural filiation.

On the contrary, when someone loses sight of the meaning of God's paternity, the fourth commandment can easily seem a burden or an annoying duty. That conflicts sometimes arise between parents and children is not surprising, but these discrepancies cannot be allowed to undermine the treasure of love and noble family traditions. Nor is it Christian to permit anyone to ridicule or disdain the role of parents. As our Father never tired of repeating, in regard to the pejorative abuse of the term "paternalism": *****You should know that the Father is paternalistic, and he loves your parents very much, and my own, and God who is my Father.***[^c07-35] ******

Although many factors are involved, the mentality of rebellion and protest against any tradition, the lack of respect and veneration for one's elders, the disrespect for all authority, and principally that of parents, stems ultimately from man's rebellion against God. When the first commandment is expunged, the fourth is immediately weakened. St. Paul speaks of *times of stress. For men will be lovers of self, lovers of money, proud, arrogant, abusive, disobedient to their parents, ungrateful, unholy, inhuman, implacable, slanderers, profligates, fierce haters of good, treacherous, reckless, swollen with conceit, lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God, holding the form of religion but denying the power of it*.[^c07-36]

Our Lord left us his clear teaching and example. While reaffirming that love for God has absolute rights, and all human loves should be subordinated to him,[^c07-37] he himself was subject to the authority of his parents.[^c07-38] He learned his trade from St. Joseph,[^c07-39] helping him to support the household. He performed the first of his miracles at his Mother's request.[^c07-40] He chose three of his disciples from among his relatives.[^c07-41] And before giving his life in reparation for our sins, he entrusted his most holy Mother to the care of St. John.[^c07-42] In addition, he worked many miracles moved by the words and tears of a mother[^c07-43] or a father.[^c07-44] As St. Paul says, parents are, by a special title, God's representatives: for from the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ *every paternity in heaven and on earth receives its name*.[^c07-45]

*Our natural parents\...are, so to say, images of the immortal God. In them we behold a picture of our own origin. From them we have received existence, and God made use of them to infuse into us a soul and reason; by them we were led to the Sacraments, instructed in our religion, schooled in right conduct and holiness, and trained in civil and human knowledge*.[^c07-46] **We could also add here countless signs of affection and abnegation that we may not even be aware of, but that have had great importance in our life. And even though a divine vocation is a strictly supernatural gift, one which is completely gratuitous, our Father often said that ***we owe ninety percent of our vocation to our parents***.[^c07-47]

Children have an immense debt of gratitude towards their parents. They owe them affection, honor, obedience and veneration; and not in a formal and detached way, as when fulfilling a simple duty of justice, but with great love and affection. When we truly love someone, *the first thing that we give them is love, by which we desire the good for them. Therefore, there is no doubt that love is rightly the first gift, through which all the other gifts are given*.[^c07-48]

### Manifestations of good filiation

*The application of this Commandment is of very great extent. Besides our natural parents, there are many others whose power, rank, usefulness, exalted functions or office, entitle them to parental honor*.[^c07-49] **Our Father reminded us that ***a family includes not only husband and wife, but also the children, and, in different degrees, the grandparents, other relatives, and even the domestic help in those households that have it. All these person should in some way share in the warmth of the home and family***.[^c07-50]

The mutual rights and duties arising from these relationships are also included in the fourth precept. Nevertheless, love for parents, in its various manifestations, is the model that determines the relationships of charity within the family and with the other persons who in one way or another share in the mission of parents, such as teachers, authorities and elders.

Among the many manifestations of affection owed to parents are: *beseeching God on their behalf, that they may lead prosperous and happy lives, beloved and esteemed by all who know them, and most pleasing in the sight of God and of the saints in heaven.*

*We also honor them by submission to their wishes and indications. "Hear my son," says Solomon, "the instruction of thy father, and forsake not the law of thy mother; that grace may be added to thy head, and a chain of gold to thy neck \...."*[^c07-51]

*We also honor our parents by the imitation of their good example; for, to seek to resemble closely anyone is the highest mark of esteem towards him. We also honor them when we not only ask, but follow their advice*.[^c07-52]

*Again we honor our parents when we relieve their necessities, supplying them with necessary food and clothing according to the words of Christ, in reproving the impiety of the Pharisees\....*[^c07-53] *This duty becomes still more imperative when they are visited by severe illness. We should then see to it that they do not neglect confession and the other Sacraments which every Christian should receive at the approach of death. We should also see that pious and religious persons visit them frequently to strengthen their weakness, assist them by their counsel, and animate them to the hope of immortality, that having risen above the concerns of this world, they may fix their thoughts entirely on God. Thus blessed with the sublime virtues of faith, hope and charity, and fortified by the helps of religion, they will not only look at death without fear, but will even welcome it, as it hastens their entrance into eternity.*

*Finally, we honor our parents, even after their death, by attending their funerals, procuring for them suitable obsequies and burial, having due suffrages and anniversary Masses offered for them, and faithfully executing their last will*.[^c07-54]

One has a special obligation towards one's parents when they are not Christians, or are separated from the faith or from friendship with God. The order of charity demands that our apostolate begin with those to whom we owe such great benefits. It should be an apostolate full of affection and respect, which consists above all in prayer and mortification and in the example of filial affection and cheerfulness. In addition, one needs to seek out occasions to bring them into contact with those who can speak to them about God with greater authority, since children cannot constitute themselves teachers of their parents on their own initiative.

Our Founder insisted: ***We have to love our parents a lot; otherwise we wouldn**'**t have the spirit of Opus Dei. Stay close to your parents. I love them a lot. We need to bring them close to the Work, which is to bring them close to God. Try to get your parents to love the Work. Let them know that we love them. How can we please God if we abandon the souls of those who have loved us so much on earth, and have contributed so much, at times without realizing it, to our vocation?***[^c07-55]

***\* \* \* \* \****

In his letter to the Ephesians, St. Paul writes: "*Honor your father and mother" (this is the first commandment with a promise), "that it may be well with you and that you may live long on the earth."*[^c07-56] **And according to St. Thomas, among the many goods that Sacred Scripture promises, two stand out: grace in our present life with glory in eternity; and a long life. For *natural life is very little in comparison with the life of grace. Therefore, if you are not grateful for the gift of life that you have received from your parents, you are not worthy of the life of grace, which is much more important, and even less of the life of glory, which is the greatest thing that we can aspire to*.[^c07-57]

On the other hand, when God promises to increase one's years on earth, we must take into account *that a life is long when it is full, and this fullness is not measured by time, but by deeds\...Therefore a full life is a virtuous life. Thus a holy and virtuous man lives a very full life, even if, according to his earthly life, he dies prematurely. As the book of Wisdom says: "Being perfected in a short time, he fulfilled long years; for his soul was pleasing to the Lord."*[^c07-58] *In the same way, we say that someone has been very successful in business, if in one day he earns what someone else earns in a year.*[^c07-59]

The promises that God wanted to attach to the fourth commandment underline the moral importance of this precept. Not only because our parents take precedence among those we are to love like ourselves, but also because this affection is, as it were, the first sign of a Christian's fidelity to Christ's commandments. *If anyone does not provide for his relatives, and especially for his own family, he has disowned the faith and is worse than an unbeliever*.[^c07-60]

When we love, honor and serve our parents, we love, honor and serve God. We are giving thanks, through his most immediate and natural representatives, for all his benefits. And we are disposing ourselves to receive new graces and gifts, because God especially rewards thankful hearts. *With all your heart honor your father, and do not forget the birth pangs of your mother*, **Holy Scripture admonishes us. *Remember that through your parents you were born; and what can you give back to them that equals their gift to you?* [^c07-61]

## GOD'S CO-WORKERS — Fourth Commandment (II) {#cuaderno.4.8}

*Just as the law of God commands children to honor, obey and respect their parents, so are there reciprocal duties which parents owe to their children. Parents are obliged to bring up their children in the knowledge and practice of religion, and to give them the best rules for the regulation of their lives; so that, instructed and trained in religion, they may serve God in a holy and steadfast manner.*[^c08-1]

*The Parents' Mission*

This second part of the fourth commandment is, as it were, the reverse side and guarantee of the duties of filial piety expressly commanded by the words of the precept: *honor your father and your mother*.[^c08-2] **By natural law, it also has greater force insofar as the work of parents has an immense influence on the formation of their children's conscience and the development of their habits. Thus St. Paul says that *children ought not to lay up for their parents, but parents for their children*.[^c08-3]

This moral duty has been established by God when he instituted marriage as the *principle and foundation of the family and, in consequence, also of the human community*.[^c08-4] **Therefore he endowed the marriage contract with certain specific goods and properties such as unity and indissolubility, so that *by its very nature the institution of marriage and married love is ordered to the procreation and education of the offspring and it is in them that it finds its crowning glory.*[^c08-5]

Among all the goods of marriage, including love, fidelity and mutual assistance, children take the first place. *Marriage and married love are by nature ordered to the procreation and education of children. Indeed children are the supreme gift of marriage and greatly contribute to the good of the parents themselves. God himself said: "It is not good that man should be alone"*[^c08-6] *and "from the beginning \[he\] made them male and female;"*[^c08-7] *wishing to associate them in a special way with his own creative work, God blessed man and woman with the words: "Be fruitful and multiply."* [^c08-8] *Without intending to underestimate the other ends of marriage, it must be said that true married love and the whole structure of family life which results from it is directed to disposing the spouses to cooperate valiantly with the love of the Creator and Savior, who through them will increase and enrich his family from day to day.*[^c08-9]

Through marriage, God calls men and women to participate in his omnipotence, wisdom and love. God counts on parents as instruments of his infinite goodness, not only so that they conceive a body into which he will infuse a spiritual soul, but also so that the new creature who has been called into existence might grow, develop, and receive all the help needed to attain to the holiness of life of the children of God.

*The blessing of offspring\...is not completed by the mere begetting of them, but something else must be added, namely the proper education of the offspring. For the most wise God would have failed to make sufficient provision for children that had been born, and so for the whole human race, if he had not given to those to whom he had entrusted the power and right to beget them, the power also and the right to educate them. For no one can fail to see that children are incapable of providing wholly for themselves, even in matters pertaining to their natural life, and much less in those pertaining to the supernatural, but require for many years to be helped, instructed, and educated by others. Now it is certain that by the law of nature and of God this right and duty of educating their offspring belongs in the first place to those who began the work of nature by giving them birth, and they are indeed forbidden to leave unfinished this work and so expose it to certain ruin.*[^c08-10]

The mission entrusted by God to parents includes much more than passing on to their children the basic tools needed to survive and prosper in this world. *Human life and its transmission are realities whose meaning is not limited by the horizons of this life only: their true evaluation and full meaning can only be understood in reference to man's eternal destiny.*[^c08-11] **Therefore the cooperation of parents has as its ultimate goal the holiness of their children. *God wishes men to be born not only that they should live and fill the earth, but much more that they may be worshippers of God, that they may know him and love him, and finally enjoy him for ever in heaven.*[^c08-12] Our Father often reminded fathers and mothers of families: ***animals also bring children into the world, but you don**'**t want to be satisfied with just that. You want to pass on to them your ideals, your faith, your clean behavior, and so many wonderful virtues that you live within your family***.[^c08-13]

By divine command, parents have the duty to *love and provide for their children, to ensure their religious and civil education, to give them good example, to keep them far from occasions of sin, to correct their defects, and to help them to find and follow the vocation to which God is calling them.*[^c08-14] Thus the mission of a mother and father includes providing the essential physical care, which begins even before the child's conception by their clean and chaste premarital behavior. It requires providing their children with a comfortable and cheerful home, and a sound education and a professional future. And it especially requires providing religious and moral instruction, striving to ensure their incorporation into the Church as soon as possible by baptism, which removes original sin and permits the Holy Spirit to take possession of the child's soul.[^c08-15]

These are grave obligations that can never be shirked. Their fulfillment affects both the temporal well being and the eternal salvation of the children God entrusts to each family. Parents who neglect their duties, aside from injuring the body and soul of their children, offend God since they refuse to collaborate in bringing to fruition the glorifying and sanctifying work begun when a new baby is brought into the world.[^c08-16] Other things being equal, the sin is so much the more serious, the greater the material and spiritual injury caused to the child, and therefore to the whole of society, by the omission of the due care.

In our Lord's teaching, children have a special place. He received and blessed them personally.[^c08-17] He held them up them as a model,[^c08-18] and he instructed us to look after them. *See that you do not despise one of these little ones; for I tell you that in heaven their angels always behold the face of my Father who is in heaven.*[^c08-19] He told us that *whoever receives this child in my name receives me,*[^c08-20] **and stated that one of the most abominable sins is scandal, which corrupts the souls of children: *whoever causes one of these little ones who believe in me to sin, it would be better for him to have a great millstone fastened round his neck and to be drowned in the depth of the sea*.[^c08-21]

## Vocational Meaning of Paternity

The task of educating children on the path towards the fullness of the glory of the children of God demands preparation and a specific grace. Our Lord grants this to Christian spouses by means of a sacrament. ***The purpose of marriage is to help married people sanctify themselves and others. For this reason they receive a special grace in the sacrament instituted by Jesus Christ. Those who are called to the married state will, with the grace of God, find within their state everything they need to be holy, to identify themselves each day more with Jesus Christ, and to lead those with whom they live to God****.*[^c08-22] **

In order to second God's wishes, which are different for each child, parents need, in addition to sacramental grace, the requisite human virtues that, like all good habits, are acquired by means of personal effort. They need to prepare themselves for the specific mission God has entrusted to them, making use of all the human and supernatural means at their disposal. Thus one will avoid the sad paradox that *the offices and professions of a transitory and earthly life, which are certainly of far less importance, are prepared for by long and careful study; whereas for the fundamental duty and obligation of educating their children many parents have little or no preparation, immersed as they are in temporal cares.*[^c08-23] **Therefore, our mother the Church has emphasized that it is of *the greatest importance that the new generations be brought up with adequate cultural as well as religious formation, as is the duty and right of parents, leading to a profound sense of responsibility in all the expressions of their life and therefore also in regard to the forming of a family and to the procreation and education of the children*.[^c08-24]

The dominant theme in this formation is to show to everyone the vocational meaning of marriage, of home life. This is one of the tasks that God wished to entrust to Opus Dei through its founder. ***Opus Dei has made of marriage a divine way, a vocation, and this has many consequences for personal holiness and for apostolate***.[^c08-25] To discover these consequences and apply them to their family's daily activity, ***it is important for married people to acquire a clear sense of the dignity of their vocation. They must know that they have been called by God not only to human love but also to a divine love, through their human love. It is important for them to realize that they have been chosen from eternity to cooperate with the creative power of God by having and then bringing up children. Our Lord asks them to make their home and their entire family life a testimony of all the Christian virtues***.[^c08-26]

By God's will, parents are the principal educators of their children. They can and often should ask for and accept the help of other persons and institutions, within and outside of the family circle, but without ever surrendering the rights they have received from God, and which they must administer with prudence and loyalty.[^c08-27]

## Education in the Home

All of the obligations that the vocation of being parents brings with it are ordered to helping their children attain the supernatural end for which they were created and redeemed. The family, by its very nature, is the first environment in which a child's personality unfolds and matures. *That education, as a rule, will be more effective and lasting which is received in a well-ordered and well-disciplined Christian family; and more efficacious in proportion to the clear and constant good example set, first by the parents, and then by the other members of the family*.[^c08-28]

Example here is decisive. Words are needed to transmit many truths and experiences that it would be difficult to communicate in any other way. But words are limited, and their ability to influence conduct is conditioned by many factors. Example has the power of what is concrete and habitual, showing that something is really attainable. Few arguments are as persuasive as contemplating in real life what is proposed as a model. Speaking to fathers and mothers, our Father insisted on the influence of example in the family: ***From the very start, children are relentless witnesses of their parents**'* *lives. You don**'**t realize it, but they judge everything, and at times they judge you in a bad light. So what happens at home influences your children for good or for bad. Try to give them good example. Don**'**t hide your piety. Act uprightly. Then they will learn, and they will be the crown of your maturity and old age. You are like an open book to them.***[^c08-29] **Children have to find in their parents' lives lessons of sincerity, of loyalty, of love for freedom and personal responsibility, of strength, hard work, cheerfulness and optimism, understanding and love.

A delicate and at the same time very important chapter is introducing one's children to the life of piety. Jesus taught us that God is our Father, and the Holy Spirit moves us to behave in accord with our condition as children of God. But parents, especially in the measure in which they live their paternity and maternity well, can facilitate or hinder the growth in their children of Christian behavior based on a sense of divine filiation. ***Experience shows in all Christian environments what good effects come from this natural and supernatural introduction to the life of piety given in the warmth of the home. Children learn to place God first and foremost in their affections. They learn to see God as their Father and Mary as their Mother and learn to pray following their parents**'* *example. In this way one can easily see what a wonderful apostolate parents have and how it is their duty to live a fully Christian life of prayer, so they can communicate their love of God to their children, which is something more than just teaching them***.[^c08-30]

The Catechism of the Council of Trent puts parents on guard against some common mistakes in the education of children. *Parents must not be too harsh with their children, in word or action. St. Paul mentions this in his letter to the Colossians: 'Fathers, do not provoke your children, lest they become discouraged.'* [^c08-31] *There is a real danger that parental severity may break a child's spirit, with the result that his whole life will be one of fear. Parents should exercise correction, not vengeance, on their children.*[^c08-32] **Our Father explained this in many practical ways; for example, he suggested that fathers and mothers must win the friendship and affection of their children: ***Listen to your children. Deal with them on a one-to-one basis, face-to-face, with lots of understanding and affection***.[^c08-33] Above all he insisted that they should not discredit their authority by becoming angry and losing their peace. ***If you ever lose your temper, hold your tongue. Otherwise, you will regret that you said anything. Later on,*** he counseled one mother, ***when you have calmed down and are in God**'**s presence, take your children aside individually and speak to them with confidence, in a tranquil tone***.[^c08-34] **Parents will know how to act correctly if they ****realize that ***the souls of your children are your foremost priority. Everything else is of secondary importance.***[^c08-35]

The Catechism also cautions against the opposite defect, excessive softness. *Parents may also err through a negligent failure to correct. Parents may fail to correct their children out of sheer laziness. In many instances, these children become lost thanks to the weakness of the parents*.[^c08-36] *****When raising your children,*** our Father told parents, ***you need to strike a balance between freedom and authority***.[^c08-37] ******A parental pedagogy that allows everything, that accepts everything, that fails to teach the limits between good and evil, between just and unjust, between what is acceptable and what is unacceptable, leads nowhere, as has been abundantly shown in practice. Freedom, in order to be fruitful and not degenerate into licence or caprice, has to submit to specific norms. ***Help your children to learn how to evaluate their behavior in God**'**s presence,*** our Father advised, ***Give them supernatural reasons so that they think things through, so that they recognize their own responsibility. Never show any distrust in them. It**'**s better to let yourselves be fooled once or twice, rather than destroying their affection and union with you***.[^c08-38]

***You ought to manage the freedom of your children according to their different ages. You shouldn**'**t treat everyone the same. Justice requires that you give extra attention to those who have extra needs, but without causing any jealousy***.[^c08-39]

All the parents' work at home should be aimed at helping the children to confront life on their own, to fend for themselves. Parents only have a limited time in which they can mold their children's character before they become independent of the protective environment of the family and are obliged to make their own decisions about their behavior and solve for themselves professional, cultural, social problems, etc. The formation children received at home will bear fruit if parents strive to inculcate authentically Christian moral criteria both in theory and in practice.

This formation has become even more urgent in our day and age with the wave of materialism and hedonism being spread by diabolically effective means that confuse and corrupt consciences. The perversion of customs in some environments makes it even more necessary to go against the current, overcoming human respects and the uproar of a systematic atheistic and anti-Christian campaign. Against this onslaught, poorly assimilated principles, and a piety that is more sentimental than theological, form a frail and precarious defense. This explains, in some cases, the sad situations found in more than a few Christian homes.

Education at home has to be aimed at infusing a solid unity of life. The Christian teaching about God, man and earthly realities has to preside over every aspect of family life. It would be a contradiction, for example, for Christian parents, when giving advice about their children's future, to put material security in first place. If they were to inculcate an anxious need to attain a secure economic situation at any cost, they could impoverish their children spiritually without realizing it, and cut short so many great ambitions characteristic of young people, which often entail a certain daring and risk. With strong language, the Catechism of the Council of Trent warns against the mistake of many people who *think only of wealth and fame as a legacy to their children. They forget about religion and morality and an honest living, and concentrate all their exhortation on vanity and greed. If their children are rich, they think they have succeeded---no matter how their children stand in the sight of God. Can anything be more criminal than that! What such parents leave their children is not really wealth but wickedness*.[^c08-40]

## The Vocation of One's Children

It is a law of life that there comes the moment when children have to leave their parents' home. Some, to start their own home: *a man leaves his father and his mother and cleaves to his wife;* [^c08-41] **others, with a higher vocation, to dedicate themselves completely to the service of God, renouncing a human love. In each case, the children begin a life independent of their parents, for which they have been prepared within their home environment. But when this moment arrives, the parents' mission is not over. They still need to advise their children with opportune counsel, and help them with human and supernatural resources appropriate to their new situation. But they cannot interfere with or coerce the children's legitimate freedom. ***Parents have no right to impose anything on their children once they have grown up. It would be an abuse to do so. They have no right to choose for us our path in life. They can give advice, and pray, but then they must leave us be. Parents have wonderful rights, but they also have the correlative duties which are just as wonderful. If they are domineering toward their grown-up children, they violate their duties and show ignorance of human dignity. Such an attitude is a vestige of feudal times.***[^c08-42]

Each person has to follow his own calling, discovering what God's will is for him. To find and follow one's particular vocation is the secret of being happy on earth and later completely happy in heaven. Nevertheless, in the objective light of the intrinsic dignity of every calling, it is a truth of faith that celibacy undertaken for the love of God is superior to marriage, and therefore brings greater fruit for the Church and for souls.[^c08-43] Parents should want for their children what God has willed for them; but in the objective order of preferences, they should desire the best good possible for them.[^c08-44] Therefore to foster the blossoming of a vocation of total dedication to God is also a mission of parents. But they should not go beyond this. It is not up to them to discern whether or not their children have such a vocation; their role is to form the consciences of their children well, to help them to find their path, without coercing their will. Above all they should ask God to grant light to their children, so that they recognize and follow what God in his loving providence has disposed for them in this life. Our Father always pointed out that the members of Opus Dei owe ***ninety percent*** of their vocation to their parents.

It can happen, however, that *only too often parents, even those who consider themselves good Catholics, seem unable to resign themselves to the vocation of their children. Such parents have no scruple in opposing the divine call with objections of all kinds. They even have recourse to means which can imperil not only the vocation to a more perfect state, but also the very conscience and the eternal salvation of those souls they ought to hold so dear.*[^c08-45] **Sometimes such opposition is due to ***a noble love which nevertheless is only human. They mistakenly put human considerations ahead of supernatural considerations. They fail to understand the grandeur of the supernatural life, and the happiness that God bestows on his servants**---**even in this world. Therefore, parents who act in this way show, in their family, a failure of the Christian spirit***.[^c08-46]

Parents need the wisdom required to allow their daughters and sons to choose their own path freely when the right moment arrives, while never failing to provide their advice and especially their prayer. This is especially so when our Lord blesses their home by calling one of their children to his service. Then, apart from many other great goods, it is as though God were confirming and accepting the educational task of parents who have known how to prepare the soul of their child so that the divine seed could germinate.

To live the demands of the fourth commandment better, parents have the shining model of the home at Nazareth. The life of our Lady and St. Joseph was entirely dedicated to Jesus. Our Lord, at their side, *increased in wisdom and in stature, and in favor with God and man.*[^c08-47] **Mary and Joseph subordinated everything to the mission that the heavenly Father had entrusted to his only-begotten Son. Joseph protected the Child and watched over him. And his Mother, Holy Mary, assisted her Son right to the final moment, accompanying him at the foot of the Cross.[^c08-48]

## THE GIFT OF LIFE — Fifth Commandment (I) {#cuaderno.4.9}

The fifth commandment of the Decalogue, *You shall not kill,*[^c09-1] **expresses God's absolute dominion over his creation: *I kill and I make alive,*[^c09-2] **he says of himself. And the psalmist concurs: *When thou hidest thy face, they are dismayed; when thou takest away their breath, they die and return to their dust. When thou sendest forth thy Spirit, they are created; and thou renewest the face of the ground.*[^c09-3]

Like any other creature, *if man exists it is because God created him through love.*[^c09-4] **His life is God's gift; even more, it is the first of the gifts that he has received, because in order to enrich his creatures with other mercies, God first must grant them being.

In the transmission of human life, parents freely cooperate with divine Providence, contributing to the conception of the body. But the spiritual and immortal soul that gives life to man is created out of nothing immediately by God at the very instant of conception, when it is united with the body.[^c09-5] Therefore *human life is sacred---all men must recognize that fact. From its very inception it reveals the creating hand of God.*[^c09-6]

### Value of human life

The simple fact of being born is already reason to give praise and thanks to our Creator. But man, in addition, has been created in God's image and likeness,[^c09-7] adopted as his child,[^c09-8] and is ordained, by God's infinite goodness, *to a supernatural end, that is, to participate in divine goods that completely surpass the comprehension of the human mind*.[^c09-9]

Our faith teaches that man has been put on earth as the custodian and lord of all creation,[^c09-10] so that he might work and give glory to God,[^c09-11] thus attaining his eternal destiny. Earthly life is no more than a transitory passage, leading to the true Life.[^c09-12] Men and women are *viatores,* travelers, whose journey finishes in very few days: *The number of a man's days is great if he reaches a hundred years. Like a drop of water from the sea and a grain of sand so are a few years in the day of eternity.*[^c09-13]

Nevertheless, this time that is so brief is also of decisive importance, because it is our access to eternal Life. But first we must be judged, when *we\...appear before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each one may receive good or evil, according to what he has done in the body.*[^c09-14] **This is the only *tempus laborandi et merendi et augendae caritatis*,[^c09-15] **the only chance we have to work, to merit, and to grow in love for God. Afterwards, Jesus tells us, *night comes, when no one can work*.[^c09-16]

Life, then, is a unique gift that we have to take very good advantage of. ****It is a mistake to give ***the years that pass an importance that they scarcely have. They only have the value*** *****that we give them by serving God.***[^c09-17] ******We see this reflected in the parable of the virgins who *took their lamps and went to meet the bridegroom.*[^c09-18] Also in the parable of the talents, which the servants were to negotiate with until their master returned; and in that of the vineyard rented out to some tenant farmers\.... Our Founder insisted: ***every moment in our life has an eternal meaning. This world, my sons, is slipping through our fingers. We can**'**t waste time, which is so short. We have to take seriously the task of our personal sanctification and our apostolic work, which our Lord has entrusted to us. We have to administer the talents we have received faithfully and loyally, with a sense of responsibility***.[^c09-19]

Although fleeting and subject to great evils and limitations, earthly life is a treasure full of possibilities. In addition, by grace we already possess a certain beginning of the great and infinite gifts that we will enjoy in heaven. As St. John reminds us, *we are God's children now, it does not yet appear what we shall be.*[^c09-20] **Hence the paradox that a Christian in this life is always aspiring to attain Life: life eternal: *For while we are still in this tent, we sigh with anxiety; not that we would be unclothed, but that we would be further clothed, so that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life.*[^c09-21]

For a Christian, there can never be lives that are useless, worthless or absurd. Every human being, including the subnormal, the insane and invalids have an immortal soul; they are children of God and have a mission to fulfill within the plans of divine Providence, and are called to eternal happiness. Physical and spiritual evils*---****hunger, thirst, heat, cold, pain, dishonor, poverty, loneliness, betrayal, slander, prison\....***[^c09-22]**---**have been assumed and given dignity by our Savior, converted into instruments of co-redemption. Therefore they are ***man**'**s treasures on earth***,[^c09-23] ******which we must never despise. We can use them to give glory to God, to make reparation for our sins and for those of all mankind, and to complete in our flesh, as St. Paul said, *what is lacking in Christ's afflictions for the sake of his body, that is, the Church*.[^c09-24] **This is the spirit of the eight beatitudes that open the sermon on the mount,[^c09-25] and this was also the proof of Jesus' saving mission, whose first beneficiaries were precisely those who were suffering: *the blind receive their sight and the lame walk, lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear, and the dead are raised up, and the poor have good news preached to them. And blessed is he who takes no offense at me*.[^c09-26]

All the calamities in this life stem, in the last resort, from the only true evil: sin. Especially death, the greatest physical evil one can suffer.[^c09-27] But *the Catholic Church* also *teaches that bodily death, from which man would have been immune if he had not sinned*,[^c09-28] *will be overcome when that wholeness which he lost through his own fault will be given once again to him by the almighty and merciful Savior. For God has called man, and still calls him, to cleave with all his being to him in sharing forever a life that is divine and free from all decay.*[^c09-29] **Attaining union with God is the only thing that matters absolutely; earthly life, even though a great gift, is not the supreme good.

Jesus taught us: *do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul; rather fear him who can destroy both soul and body in hell*.[^c09-30] **And he said: *whoever would save his life will lose it, and whoever loses his life for my sake will find it*.[^c09-31] And even more explicitly, he reminded us that *it is better for you to enter life maimed or lame than with two hands or two feet to be thrown into the eternal fire\...it is better for you to enter life with one eye than with two eyes to be thrown into the hell of fire*.[^c09-32]

### Content and range of the precept

It is never licit for anyone to commit a crime against the life or physical integrity of himself or another person, since human life is a sovereign right of God. The natural law, by demanding the sacrosanct respect for human life expressed by the fifth commandment, leads man to recognize and accept the supreme and exclusive dominion of the Creator.

Life, which only God can grant, must never be taken away by creatures. This is the basis for the inviolable and inalienable right all men have *to life, to bodily integrity, to the means necessary for a decent standard of living.*[^c09-33] **It is a right no one can impair without committing a grave offense against the Creator and an injustice towards one's neighbor.[^c09-34]

To emphasize the importance of the goods protected by the Law's fifth precept, God revealed himself in the Old Testament as the jealous guardian of its observance. As early as the first homicide in history, he said to Cain: *The voice of your brother's blood is crying to me from the ground. And now you are cursed*.[^c09-35] **After the flood that was sent to punish mankind in its depraved state, he reminded Noah, as the representative of the human race: *For your lifeblood I will surely require a reckoning; of every beast I will require it and of man*.[^c09-36] **And later, upon sealing his covenant with the chosen people, he established that transgressions against the fifth commandment be punished with the maximum penalty.[^c09-37]

For centuries, the precept of not killing was understood by the Jews in a restrictive sense; while the law of *talion*[^c09-38] was at least sufficient to protect against unjust homicide. But our Lord, in whom the law and the prophets found their fulfillment,[^c09-39] explained the full content of the divine command, showing that it included the requirements of charity towards one's neighbor. *You have heard that it was said to the men of old, 'You shall not kill; and whoever kills shall be liable to judgment.' But I say to you that every one who is angry with his brother shall be liable to judgment; whoever insults his brother shall be liable to the council, and whoever says, 'You fool!' shall be liable to the hell of fire.*[^c09-40] **And further on: *You have heard that it was said 'You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy.' But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you, so that you may be sons of your Father who is in heaven; for he makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good, and sends rain on the just and on the unjust*.[^c09-41]

*In the explanation of this commandment,* the Catechism of the Council of Trent notes, *the Lord points out its twofold obligation. The one is prohibitory and forbids us to kill; the other is mandatory and commands us to cherish sentiments of charity and friendship towards our enemies, to have peace with all men, and finally, to endure with patience every inconvenience.*[^c09-42] Thus what in the Mosaic law was ordained principally to the conservation of physical life,[^c09-43] in the Christian dispensation is transformed into a commandment that moves one to love. It spurs us to seek peaceful communion with our neighbor; to bear injustice with meekness, eliminating all sentiments of vengeance; to overcome with understanding the frictions and problems that can arise in life. Our Lord reacts forcefully when his apostles suggest he punish a city that refuses to receive them: *he turned and rebuked them*.[^c09-44] **The spirit of Christ, which is one of love and peace, ***seeks, above all, to respect and understand each person for what he is, in his intrinsic dignity as a man and child of God***.[^c09-45] ******Therefore it excludes violence,[^c09-46] anger, revenge, rancor, hatred, fanaticism[^c09-47]---all vices that arise from self-love, from pride, and that separate a person from God and from his neighbor.

Respect for God's will and the order of charity itself (which requires caring for one's own life in order to be better able to attend to others[^c09-48]) impose not only the right but also the duty to preserve one's own existence, and the other goods inseparably united to it, in the face of the dangers that threaten it.

Legitimate defense, both for the individual and society, is a natural right, sanctioned by the Lord in the Old Testament[^c09-49] and by the magisterium of the Church,[^c09-50] even to the extent of causing the death of an unjust aggressor. Nevertheless, it should be exercised with the due caution and conditions required by the moral law,[^c09-51] because even though a person may have forfeited his right to life, the death of someone who is unjustly trying to cause grave injury to others is always something bad, which one should seek to avoid with all possible just means.[^c09-52]

Along these same lines, Catholic doctrine recognizes that legitimate civil authority has a right to deprive a transgressor of his life, as a right belonging to its ordinary competence, always assuming that it is exercised for the gravest reasons, and with due juridical precautions.[^c09-53] The Church's magisterium teaches that the death penalty is not contrary to the natural law;[^c09-54] but neither does it arise necessarily from that law: it is a question of suitability, which depends on circumstances. Thus a Catholic can defend, based on cultural and social circumstances and a varying evaluation of the same, both the need for the death penalty and the desirability of abolishing it, within a specific juridical order.

Aside from these cases (legitimate defense and the death penalty), *all other killing is forbidden, whether we consider the person who kills, the person killed, or the means used to kill.*

*As to the person who kills, the Commandment recognizes no exception whatever, be he rich or powerful, master or parent. All, without exception or distinction, are forbidden to kill.*

*With regard to the person killed, the law extends to all. There is no individual, however humble or lowly his condition, whose life is not shielded by this law.*

*It also forbids suicide. No man possesses such power over his own life as to be at liberty to put himself to death. Hence we find that the Commandment does not say: "Thou shalt not kill another," but simply: "Thou shalt not kill."*

*Finally if we consider the numerous means by which murder may be committed, the law admits of no exception. Not only does it forbid to take away the life of another by laying violent hands on him, by means of a sword, a stone, a stick, a halter, or by administering poison; but also strictly prohibits the accomplishment of the death of another by counsel, assistance, help or any other means whatever.*[^c09-55]
